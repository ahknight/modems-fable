Epilogue · What came alongside, and after

The silence, and what it inherited

The technologies that replaced dial-up did not make a sound when they connected. But they connected in the same way, for the same reasons, using ideas with the same names, often written by the same people.

Dial-up ran out of road because of a self-imposed handicap: it treated the connection as a telephone call. A call is a 3,000-hertz-wide channel digitized at 64,000 bits per second, and 56k had extracted very nearly all of that. To go faster, data had to stop pretending to be a voice. Three wired technologies did that, each in its own way. (Wireless is another story, for another site.)

ISDN: the digital line that came too early

The telephone companies’ own answer predates everything in the last four chapters. If the network is digital inside, extend the digits all the way to the customer. The Integrated Services Digital Network’s basic service delivered two 64,000 bit/s “B” channels and a 16,000 bit/s signaling channel over an ordinary copper pair{{cite itu-i412}}. Use both B channels together and you had 128,000 bit/s.

The engineering inside was familiar. The American standard for ISDN’s copper loop uses a four-level signal with echo cancellation to carry both directions on one pair{{cite ansi-t1601}}, the V.32 idea applied to a wider band. That standard already uses a phrase that would outlive it: it calls the loop a “digital subscriber line”{{cite ansi-t1601}}.

In American homes ISDN mostly failed. Users and equipment makers blamed the phone companies’ pricing and foot-dragging, and by the spring of 1996 the alternatives below were already in trials{{cite cnet-1996-04-isdn}}. The industry joke, recorded by CNET in 1996, was that the letters stood for “It Still Does Nothing”{{cite cnet-1996-04-isdn}}. Elsewhere it did rather more: Germany had the highest ISDN penetration in the world by 1998{{cite telecompaper-1998-isdn}}. And the idea behind it had a quiet second life in 56k: a digital connection to the telephone network, of the kind ISDN offered, is precisely what V.90 requires at the provider’s end{{cite itu-v90}}.

DSL: the rest of the wire

The copper pair to your house can carry frequencies far above the 4,000 Hz the telephone network uses; it is the converter at the telephone office that throws them away. Put a different box at the office end, and the same wire has far more bandwidth to offer.

The difficulty is that phone wires travel in thick bundles and fast signals leak from one pair into its neighbors. The idea that made consumer DSL practical is credited to Joseph Lechleider of Bellcore, in the late 1980s: make the service lopsided. If nearly all the high-speed traffic flows toward the home, the worst kind of interference largely disappears{{cite nyt-lechleider}}. Hence the “A” in ADSL, asymmetric. The phone companies’ original interest, the same obituary notes, was delivering video, not the Internet; and Lechleider recalled a long season of doubt: “There were people who didn’t want to deploy it. There were people who didn’t think it would work”{{cite nyt-lechleider}}.

How to signal on such a wire was decided in what one participant, John Cioffi of Stanford, calls the “Bellcore Transmission Olympics” of 1993. The winning design was his group’s discrete multitone: divide the band into hundreds of narrow channels, measure each one when the line starts up, and load each with as many bits as it can carry{{cite cioffi-ch4 ethw-adsl}}. The first American standard followed in 1995 and the ITU’s in 1999, at roughly 6 million bit/s toward the home{{cite ethw-adsl itu-g9921}}. Later generations were specified for as much as 200 million bit/s, both directions combined (VDSL2, 2006), and a billion (G.fast, 2014), using ever more of the wire’s bandwidth{{cite itu-g9932 itu-g9701}}.

“DSL allowed the phone companies to milk another two decades out of their copper infrastructure.”

Craig Moffett, telecommunications analyst, quoted in the New York Times, 2015{{cite nyt-lechleider}}

Cable: the other wire into the house

The other wire into most American homes was cable television’s. Rouzbeh Yassini’s company LANcity built early modems for it, and he remembered the first generation as “half the size of refrigerators”{{cite yassini-oral}}. The cable industry’s common standard is DOCSIS, whose core radio-frequency specification is dated March 26, 1997{{cite docsis-rfi-1997}}. Its downstream signal is a 64- or 256-point QAM constellation in a channel as wide as one television station; upstream, the humbler 4- and 16-point constellations of the 1980s{{cite docsis-rfi-1997 itu-j112}}. By its own specifications’ ceilings the family has grown from 40 million bit/s to 10 billion{{cite cablelabs-docsis}}.

The long tail

In the Pew Research Center’s surveys, dial-up at home peaked in April 2001, at 41 percent of American adults. Broadband passed it between the spring of 2004 and the spring of 2005. By 2013 dial-up was down to 3 percent{{cite pew-chart pew-2013}}.

It did not go to zero. The Census Bureau’s household survey counted about 1.2 million American households in 2013 whose only Internet subscription was dial-up. In 2023 it counted 163,401, and in 2025, the most recent year available, 111,016{{cite census-b28002}}. Those are survey estimates, with margins of error from about 6,000 to 16,000 households.

British Telecom switched off its dial-up service in 2013{{cite bbc-2013-bt}}. America Online, which by late 1997 had more than 600,000 modems answering its phones{{cite ap-1997-aol-10m}}, announced in August 2025 that “AOL routinely evaluates its products and services and has decided to discontinue Dial-up Internet,” effective September 30{{cite aol-notice cnn-2025-aol}}.

The sound itself outlived its usefulness long before that. Recall why you could hear it at all: a setting, M1, that left the modem’s speaker on until the connection was made, so that a person could tell a busy signal from a success. A modem is a machine for carrying signals no human was meant to hear, and for one generation, by an accident of a default setting, tens of millions of people listened to it think. Every part of what it was thinking is still in use. It has only stopped saying it out loud.
