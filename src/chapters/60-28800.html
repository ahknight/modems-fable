Chapter 6 · The modem that measures the line

28,800bits per secondFirst, ask the wire what it can do

Every earlier modem was designed for an imaginary average phone line. V.34 stopped guessing. Its handshake is an experiment, performed on your particular call, and it is why the sound finally became the one everybody remembers.

The road there

Work on the successor to V.32 bis began under the nickname V.fast. The aim was at least to double 14,400, and Dale Walsh of U.S. Robotics, who sat on the committee, wrote that participants first thought 28,800 was a stretch{{cite walsh-1994}}. The schedule slipped by about a year in 1993{{cite telecompaper-1993-vfast}}, and the industry would not wait. That August, Rockwell began selling chip sets for its own interim scheme, “V.Fast Class,” and modem makers shipped it{{cite telecompaper-1993-vfc}}. These modems reached 28,800 only when calling others of the same make-up{{cite walsh-1994}}, and when the real standard was approved on September 20, 1994{{cite itu-v34-1994}}, owners whose V.FC modems could not be upgraded were stranded{{cite nyt-1996-04-manes}}.

The standard itself combined contributions from many companies. Four of the engineers involved later wrote:

“Indeed, the technology in V.34 is superior to that proposed by any single company, and in that sense V.34 may be regarded as a triumph of the standards process.”

G. D. Forney Jr., L. Brown, M. V. Eyuboglu and J. L. Moran III, IEEE Communications Magazine, December 1996{{cite forney-v34}}

What it took

The single biggest gain, those authors say, came from an idea that sounds almost too simple: use all the bandwidth the line actually has{{cite forney-v34}}. V.32 and V.32 bis send 2,400 symbols a second around an 1,800 Hz carrier on every call, a choice safe enough for a poor line and wasteful on a good one. V.34 has six symbol rates, from 2,400 up to 3,429 a second, and a choice of carrier frequency for each{{cite itu-v34}}. A wider signal carries proportionally more.

But you cannot know how wide a signal a given call will pass until you try. So V.34 tries. In the middle of the handshake each modem plays the other a chord of 21 pure tones, spaced 150 Hz apart from 150 to 3,750 Hz, and the listener measures how strongly each arrives and how much noise lies between them{{cite itu-v34}}. This is line probing. From the result the two modems choose a symbol rate, a carrier, a transmit power and a degree of pre-emphasis to suit this call and no other.

Around that central idea, V.34 gathers nearly every refinement that communications theory had produced{{cite forney-v34}}:

  • A stronger trellis code. The mandatory code is a 16-state design by Lee-Fang Wei that works on pairs of symbols at a time, so in four dimensions instead of two; optional 32- and 64-state codes go further.
  • Shell mapping. If outer dots of a constellation, which cost the most power, are used a little less often than inner ones, the same power buys more margin, about 0.8 dB. Forney and his co-authors credit the technique to “many authors” across several companies and universities.
  • Precoding. Part of the equalizer moves into the transmitter, which pre-distorts the signal so the line’s distortion undoes it. The principle goes back to Tomlinson and Harashima around 1971; V.34’s version emerged from a string of competing proposals by Motorola, General DataComm, AT&T and academic researchers.
  • Fractional bits. Dividing bits unevenly among groups of symbols lets the modem use, say, 8.4 bits per symbol, so that it can land on a multiple of 2,400 bit/s whatever the symbol rate{{cite itu-v34}}.

All of this is arithmetic, and a great deal of it. Walsh estimated 1,000 to 2,000 computations for every bit transferred{{cite walsh-1994}}. Forney, a year after the standard, could still hardly believe where that had ended up:

“Now an extremely complicated V.34 modem going at 28,800 bits per second, using every last dollop of technology that we know about, doing tens of millions of digital signal processing operations per second, is down at your local computer store for 299 bucks.”

G. David Forney Jr., IEEE oral history, May 1995{{cite forney-oral}}

The handshake, in four phases

This is the sound. If you used the Internet between 1994 and the arrival of broadband, most of what you heard on each call is in the player below. The standard divides it into four phases{{cite itu-v34 itu-v8}}.

Phase 1: who are you? A problem had been building since 2,400 bit/s: a modem might now support a dozen modulations, and trying each in turn would take a minute. V.8, approved the same day as V.34, replaces guesswork with a menu. The answerer sends the familiar 2,100 Hz answer tone, with its phase-reversal ticks, but makes it flutter, its loudness wobbling fifteen times a second. An old modem hears an ordinary answer tone. A V.8 caller hears the flutter, knows it has reached a modern modem, and sends its “call menu”: a short digital message listing everything it can do. The answerer returns the “joint menu” of what they share{{cite itu-v8}}. Both messages are sent at 300 bits per second in V.21, the two-tone warble of 1964, because that is the one language no modem ever forgot.

Phase 2: probing. Two short bursts at 600 bit/s exchange each transmitter’s abilities. Then each modem holds a steady tone, 1,200 Hz from the caller and 2,400 Hz from the answerer, and they flip them back and forth to time the round trip, the sonar trick inherited from V.32. Then the chord: loud for 160 ms, softer for up to half a second more, first from the answerer, then from the caller. Then two more short bursts to compare notes.

Phase 3: training. At the symbol rate just chosen, the answerer sends a brief rasp, a short sweep-like sequence designed to excite every frequency equally, and then a long hiss on four points while the caller’s equalizer learns the line and the answerer learns its own echo. Then they swap.

Phase 4: final offer. Both transmit at once. Each sends a small frame of “modulation parameters” naming the highest speed it believes it can receive. The lower figure wins, the constellations expand, and the speaker clicks off.

What changed from 14,400. The opening tone now flutters. A menu exchange in 300-baud tones replaces trial and error. Probing is entirely new. The symbol rate and carrier are chosen during the call instead of fixed in advance. Training is longer. And the constellation at the end is far denser: 896 points in this example, against 128.

Required and optional. Every V.34 modem must support symbol rates of 2,400, 3,000 and 3,200; the other three, including the fastest, are optional. The 16-state trellis code is mandatory and the larger ones optional. Speeds may differ in the two directions if both modems allow it. V.8 is the mandatory way in{{cite itu-v34}}.

What is approximated here. Everything through Phase 4 is built from the standard’s definitions. The data that follows is not: real V.34 chooses each point using shell mapping, the 4D trellis code and precoding together, which is most of the standard’s length. We draw points at random from the correct constellation at the correct symbol rate, which looks and sounds right and carries no data. The timeline marks it with stripes.

Sidebar

Playing games over a phone call

The simplest multiplayer game network is two modems and no network at all. One player’s computer dials, the other’s answers, and the game sends its own messages over the raw connection. DOOM gained this in version 1.2, on February 17, 1994, two months after its release; the original could play only over an office-style local network{{cite doom-faq}}. Two players, 9,600 bit/s or better, one of you typing the other’s phone number after a -dial switch{{cite doom-faq doom-readme}}. For more than two you needed a service such as DWANGO, which ran its own banks of modems in cities around the United States so that players could dial a local number, and which closed in 1998{{cite ign-1998-dwango}}.

Playing over the Internet was harder, because the Internet added delay on top of the modems’. John Carmack designed Quake’s networking in 1996 from a house with a fast leased line, and discovered what everyone else’s connection was like:

“Unfortunately, 99% of the world gets on with a slip or ppp connection over a modem, often through a crappy overcrowded ISP. This gives 300+ ms latencies, minimum. Client. User’s modem. ISP’s modem. Server. ISP’s modem. User’s modem. Client. God, that sucks.”

John Carmack, .plan file, August 2, 1996{{cite carmack-1996-08}}

His fix, released that December as QuakeWorld, was to stop making the player wait for the server’s answer. The game on your computer predicts where you will be and draws that immediately, correcting itself when the server disagrees{{cite carmack-1996-08 carmack-1996-12}}. Client-side prediction, invented to hide the modem, is how nearly every online action game has worked since. Carmack also had the server ration each dial-up player to about 2,000 bytes a second, since a modem could deliver no more{{cite carmack-1996-08}}. Players sorted themselves into castes by connection. By 1999 Carmack could write “LPB” and “HPB” in his public notes without explaining them{{cite carmack-1999}}: by the usual reading, players with low “ping” times on campus or office lines, and everyone on a modem.
