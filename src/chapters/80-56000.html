The insight

Go back to the last chapter’s picture of a phone call: analog wire from your house, a converter at the telephone office that turns voltage into 8,000 numbers a second, a digital network, and at the far end a second converter turning numbers back into voltage for another analog wire. The rounding error of the first converter is the “noise” that caps an analog modem near 35,000 bit/s.

By the mid-1990s, one kind of customer had no analog wire. A large Internet provider could connect its modem pool to the telephone network digitally, so that the numbers flowed straight into its equipment with no wire and no converter in between. On a call from your house to such a provider there is only one conversion between analog and digital, at your own telephone office{{cite itu-pr-1998-02}}.

Now think about the direction from the provider to you. The provider’s equipment can choose exactly which number to put into the network for each of the 8,000 sample slots. The network delivers those numbers untouched to the converter serving your line, which turns each into a precise, known voltage. Nothing has been measured and rounded off, so no rounding noise has been added. The telephone network’s own set of voltage levels has become the modem’s alphabet{{cite ayanoglu-1998}}. That is PCM modulation, after pulse-code modulation, the telephone industry’s name for its number stream.

Eight thousand symbols a second, each one of up to 256 levels, would be 64,000 bit/s. Not all of that is usable, for reasons that sources weight differently. The levels are deliberately crowded together near zero, where speech needs fine gradations, and the smallest ones are too close for a modem at the end of a long wire to tell apart. Some networks borrow the least important bit of occasional samples for their own signaling, and some quietly rescale the numbers to adjust volume. And the total power a modem may put on a line is capped by regulation. What survives is about seven bits per sample: 56,000{{cite itu-v90 eetimes-gao-1998 fcc-98-221 nyt-1997-03-dunn}}.

In the other direction nothing has changed. Your modem’s signal still arrives at a converter that measures and rounds it, so upstream stays V.34, at 33,600 or less{{cite itu-v90 eetimes-gao-1998}}. A 56k connection is two different modems sharing a call. Shannon’s theorem was never broken, and the New York Times, having implied otherwise, printed a correction: the new modems would exploit “the increased use of digital circuits by telephone companies and Internet service providers. They will not circumvent Shannon’s Law”{{cite nyt-1996-09}}.

Who thought of it

Disputed The credit for 56k is genuinely contested, and was litigated. What follows is what each side’s documents say.

Bell Labs researchers were studying the idea in the early 1990s. Kalet, Mazo and Saltzberg presented a paper titled “The Capacity of PCM Voiceband Channels” at a conference in 1993{{cite kms-icc93}}, AT&T filed patent applications on modems synchronized to the network’s converters starting in October 1992{{cite att-patent-437}}, and a later Bell Labs paper says a prototype was demonstrated there in 1993 and credits Robert Lucky, the equalizer’s inventor, with first seeing the potential{{cite ayanoglu-1998}}.

Brent Townshend, an independent inventor, filed his own application on December 9, 1994, which issued as U.S. patents in 1998{{cite townshend-patent}}. He has said the idea came to him in 1993 while building a device to deliver music over phone lines{{cite nw-2004-townshend}}. His patent argues that the decisive step was the asymmetric arrangement, one end digital, and quotes the 1993 paper’s own doubt about whether the scheme could be built: “This is a hard practical problem and we can only conjecture if a reasonable solution would be possible”{{cite townshend-patent}}. He licensed U.S. Robotics in April 1996{{cite wired-1997-10}}. When his claim surfaced in the standards committee, a member recalled, “It really created an enormous mess”{{cite nw-2004-townshend}}. He sued Rockwell in 1997, alleging it had used what he showed it in confidence; Rockwell denied that, and settled in 2001 on undisclosed terms{{cite wired-1997-10 nw-2003-townshend}}. We found no court ruling on who invented what.

Sidebar

The 56k standards war

In September 1996 several companies announced 56k plans within days of one another{{cite cnet-1996-09 nyt-1996-09}}. There were two incompatible designs. U.S. Robotics called its version x2, announced it formally on October 16 with more than thirty Internet providers signed up, and began shipping modems on February 24, 1997{{cite cnet-1996-10 cnet-1997-02}}. Rockwell and Lucent, both makers of modem chips, merged their two efforts into K56flex, which reached the market weeks later after chip problems{{cite cisco-1997 cnet-1997-04}}.

An x2 modem calling a K56flex provider connected at 33,600 or below, like any V.34 modem. So did the reverse. You had to know which kind your provider had bought, providers had to guess which kind their customers would buy, and buyers held back{{cite cnet-1998-02 wired-1998-02}}. A Rockwell marketing director put it without spin:

“How confusing is this for the consumer? Very, very confusing.”

Michael Henderson, Rockwell, 1997{{cite moore-1997}}

The ITU began work on a common standard in March 1997 and reached technical agreement on February 6, 1998, eleven months later. The study group’s chairman called it “the shortest period of time ever taken” for a modem Recommendation to reach that stage{{cite itu-pr-1998-02}}. The result, V.90, took pieces from both camps and was formally approved on September 25, 1998{{cite itu-v90}}. By that October the ITU said more than 20 million V.90 modems had been supplied{{cite itu-1998-10}}.

Why you never saw 56,000

In the United States, a rule dating from 1975 limits the power of signals sent into the telephone network to an average of −12 dBm. Using the highest PCM levels freely would exceed it. The FCC’s own estimate of the resulting ceiling was 54,000 bit/s; modem makers’ manuals said 53,000{{cite fcc-98-221 usr-guide-2000}}. In September 1998 the FCC proposed relaxing the limit{{cite fr-1998}}; we did not find a final order.

That was the best case. Real connections, a 1998 report said, usually landed in the low 40,000s{{cite cnet-1998-09}}. And for some people 56k never worked at all, because the whole scheme depends on there being exactly one analog-to-digital conversion on the call. If the telephone company had installed equipment between its office and your street that converted your line to digital and back again, or if you were behind an office switchboard that did, the levels arrived rounded off, and the modems fell back to V.34{{cite usr-guide-2000 nyt-1997-03-dunn}}. For the same reason, two home 56k modems calling each other get 33,600 at best.

The handshake, with a twist

V.90’s designers could not know in advance whether a given call would support PCM, so the handshake is V.34’s with extra questions asked along the way{{cite itu-v90}}.

Phase 1 is V.8’s fluttering answer tone and 300-baud menus again, with two extra items on each menu: “I am a PCM modem” and “I am connected digitally” or “I am not.” An answering modem may precede it with a quiet two-note chirp, the greeting of an optional protocol called V.8 bis{{cite itu-v8 itu-v8bis}}.

Phase 2 is V.34’s probing: tones, flips, and the 21-tone chord in each direction. There is one small, strange change. In V.34 the caller uses the lower tone, 1,200 Hz, and the answerer the higher, 2,400 Hz. In V.90 the digital modem takes the caller’s part and the analog modem the answerer’s, although it was the analog modem that placed the call{{cite itu-v90}}. Listen for it: your modem, the caller, is on the high tone.

Phase 3 is where the new sounds are. Your modem trains the provider’s exactly as a V.34 modem would. Then the provider’s modem transmits for the first time in PCM, and what it sends is nearly a diagnostic procedure. First a buzz made of one level alternating in a fixed six-sample pattern, then a hiss made of just two voltages, one positive and one negative, so your modem can find the timing of the network’s 8,000-per-second clock and train an equalizer. Then comes the digital impairment learning sequence. Your modem has sent a list of which levels it wants to see; the provider’s modem plays them back, one after another, each alongside a reference; and your modem measures what actually arrives. This is how it discovers stolen bits and rescaled volume, and decides which levels are far enough apart to use on this line.

Phase 4: your modem tells the provider’s exactly which levels to use for each sample, the two confirm speeds, and data begins.

What changed from 33,600. Downstream, everything: no carrier, no constellation in the old sense, no trellis code, just a level chosen 8,000 times a second, visible in the lens as a ladder of dots instead of a cloud. Upstream, nothing. In the handshake: two extra menu items, a swap of roles in Phase 2, and the new PCM training and learning signals in Phase 3.

Required and optional. A V.90 modem must be able to fall back to V.34, and does so on its own if the learning sequence shows a second conversion in the path. Downstream speeds from 28,000 to 56,000 in steps of 1,333⅓ bit/s are all part of the standard; which one you get is your modem’s decision, call by call{{cite itu-v90}}. The V.8 bis greeting is optional.

What is approximated here. Phases 1 and 2, everything your modem sends in Phase 3, and the provider’s first PCM signals are built from the standards, the last of them sample by sample from the telephone network’s level table. The contents of the learning sequence were each modem maker’s private choice, so ours is an invented example with the standard’s structure. The provider’s Phase 4 signals and all data in both directions are stand-ins with the right levels, rate and power but no real coding. The stripes on the timeline show exactly where.

One more turn

V.92, approved in November 2000, applied the PCM idea to the upstream direction too, for up to 48,000 bit/s from your modem; added a quicker start-up, expected to cut the handshake from about twenty seconds to under ten; and added “modem on hold,” so that an incoming voice call need not end the connection{{cite itu-v92 itu-news-v92 edn-v92}}. Its companion V.44 was a new compression method based on an algorithm from Hughes Network Systems{{cite itu-news-v92}}. As far as we can find, nothing faster followed. The chairman of the ITU working party said at the time that he expected voiceband modems to have a long life yet{{cite itu-news-v92}}. He was right about the modems. It was the telephone call that was about to become optional.
