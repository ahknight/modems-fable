Prologue · 1949–1976

The telephone company’s wires, and nobody else’s

For twenty years the modem existed, worked, and was not yours to own. The first home modems had to get around a rule before they could get around a problem.

A modem turns bits into sound and sound back into bits, so that a network built to carry the human voice can carry data instead. The idea is older than the microchip. The Computer History Museum traces it to 1949, when a group under Jack Harrington at the Air Force Cambridge Research Center built one to send radar signals over telephone lines; the technique was adapted for the SAGE air-defense computers in 1953{{cite chm-timeline}}. The Bell System supplied SAGE’s data links, and in February 1958 it began offering a version to ordinary businesses in Illinois, Michigan and New York under the name Dataphone{{cite blr-1958-dataphone bell-transmission}}. Its house journal was not shy about it:

“Dataphone, which could be one of the most significant business developments of our times, is a high-speed transmission service for sending and receiving data over regular telephone lines.”

Bell Laboratories Record, April 1958{{cite blr-1958-dataphone}}

The machine that matters for our story arrived a few years later. Bell Labs’ own history says its first commercial modems, “developed in the early 1960s,” were the 103, 202 and 201{{cite bell-commsci}}. The Bell 103 was the slow one, 300 bits per second, but it could do something precious: carry data in both directions at once over one ordinary dialed call{{cite att-103a-1967}}. It did this with four whistles. The modem that placed the call sent a tone of 1,270 Hz to mean 1 and 1,070 Hz to mean 0. The modem that answered used 2,225 Hz and 2,025 Hz{{cite pe-pennywhistle byte-1977-09-mr-ad}}. The two pairs sit in different parts of the voice band, so each end can filter out its own voice and hear only the other’s. Remember those numbers. They will still be on the line in 1998.

Disputed Many histories say AT&T’s first commercial modem was a 110 bit/s “Bell 101” derived from SAGE in 1958, and that the 103 followed in 1962. We could not find a primary source for either statement. AT&T’s own histories put the SAGE links at 1,600 or 2,000 bit/s (the two volumes disagree) and the 1958 commercial data set at 1,000 bit/s{{cite bell-transmission bell-commsci}}; they date the 103 only to the “early 1960s.” The year 1962 comes from later journalism{{cite pcworld-2011}}, and no individual designer of the 103 is named in anything we read.

“By induction or otherwise”

A 103 was a gray cabinet weighing about 18 pounds{{cite att-103a-1967}}, and you could not buy one. You rented it from the telephone company, because the telephone company’s tariff, the legal terms of your service, forbade connecting anything else:

“No equipment, apparatus, circuit or device not furnished by the telephone company shall be attached to or connected with the facilities furnished by the telephone company, whether physically, by induction or otherwise.”

AT&T tariff F.C.C. No. 132, as quoted by the FCC in Carterfone, 1968{{cite carterfone-fcc}}

The rule was enforced against things far humbler than modems. The Hush-A-Phone was a plastic cup that snapped onto a mouthpiece so the person at the next desk could not overhear you; the phone companies threatened to disconnect people who used one. In 1956 a federal appeals court found that absurd, in a sentence that became the foundation of everything that followed: a subscriber has a right to use the telephone “in ways which are privately beneficial without being publicly detrimental”{{cite hushaphone}}.

AT&T rewrote the tariff narrowly and kept banning anything that interconnected with the line. That is what Thomas Carter of Dallas ran into. His Carterfone, sold from 1959, let a person on a two-way radio talk to a person on the telephone by cradling the handset in a box that listened and spoke to it acoustically. In June 1968 the Federal Communications Commission struck the ban down, calling it “unreasonable, discriminatory, and unlawful”{{cite carterfone-fcc}}.

“No one entity need provide all interconnection equipment for our telephone system any more than a single source is needed to supply the parts for a space probe.”

FCC, Carterfone decision, June 26, 1968{{cite carterfone-fcc}}

Why the handset went into rubber cups

Carterfone is often remembered as the day you could plug in your own modem. It was not. The decision let the phone company keep “reasonable standards,” and within months AT&T’s new tariff required a rented “protective connecting arrangement” between any customer-owned device and the wire{{cite litton}}. Bell’s own history summarizes the result: from 1968 to 1975 you could attach a non-Bell modem only through a Bell-furnished coupler{{cite bell-transmission}}. In Georgia in 1978 that coupler cost $5.20 a month plus $11.60 to install{{cite hayes-80103a-manual}}.

There was one way to connect without connecting. If your modem had a small loudspeaker and a microphone, and you pressed an ordinary telephone handset against them, no wire of yours touched the network at all. That is the acoustic coupler, and it is why a generation’s picture of “going online” is a handset jammed into two rubber cups.

Its best-documented origin is not in computing. Robert Weitbrecht, a deaf physicist at the Stanford Research Institute, built one so that deaf people could type to each other on surplus teletypewriters; he and James Marsters made the first long-distance call that way in 1964{{cite gallaudet-lang sri-heritage}}. A colleague, John Van Geen, independently designed a coupler for the Bell 103’s tones, and in January 1967 the firm Anderson-Jacobson was formed to manufacture it{{cite sri-heritage}}.

Disputed “Who invented the acoustic coupler” has no clean answer. SRI’s history calls Weitbrecht “probably the first person to conceive of an acoustic modem” and gives August 1966 for his patent filing{{cite sri-heritage weitbrecht-patent}}. But the Carterfone itself coupled to a handset acoustically from 1959, for voice{{cite carterfone-fcc}}, and the Computer History Museum holds a Livermore Data Systems data coupler it dates to “circa 1965”{{cite chm-livermore}}.

The last lock opened slowly. In November 1975 the FCC adopted a registration program, Part 68 of its rules: instead of renting a protective box, a manufacturer could have its equipment certified once and let customers plug it straight into the wall{{cite litton}}. The telephone companies fought it; a federal appeals court upheld it in March 1977{{cite ncuc-fcc}}. Sources we read give 1976, 1977 and 1978 for when a registered modem could first legally be plugged in, depending on which milestone they count.

So by the middle of the 1970s all the pieces existed: a simple, well-understood 300 bit/s signaling scheme; a way to reach the phone line without the phone company’s permission; and, newly, hobbyists with computers of their own. What did not exist was a modem any of them could afford.
