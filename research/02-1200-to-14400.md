# Fact sheet 02 — 1200, 2400, 9600 and 14,400 bit/s (VA3400, Bell 212A, V.22, V.22bis, MNP, V.42/V.42bis, PEP, HST, V.32, V.32bis)

Research date: 2026-11-20. Every source below was opened and read during this task (direct fetch, PDF text extraction, or archive.org OCR text of the scanned issue). Nothing is filled in from memory; where memory would have been needed, the item is in "Could not verify".

**Confidence tags**

- **HIGH** — primary or authoritative source read directly (ITU/CCITT text, the paper itself, an oral-history or interview transcript, a company filing or press release, a contemporary news report of the event).
- **MEDIUM** — reputable secondary source read directly (later trade-press survey, review, glossary, obituary).
- **LOW** — could not fully verify; the reason is stated.

**Reading notes**

- Excerpts are verbatim and at most 40 words. `[...]` marks an omission, `[sic]` an error in the source.
- Magazine and newspaper sources (Computerworld, PC Magazine, InfoWorld, Network World, BYTE, BSTJ) were read as archive.org OCR text (`<identifier>_djvu.txt`). In excerpts, line-break hyphens are removed and obvious OCR character damage is repaired (for example `1 ,200` → `1,200`). No passage with ambiguous OCR is quoted. Check the page scan before publishing a quote.
- archive.org URLs have the form `https://archive.org/details/<identifier>`. Page numbers are given only where the OCR shows a folio.
- ITU PDFs are the "Blue Book" re-editions (11/88) for V.22, V.22bis, V.29, V.32 and V.42. The line under each title lists the Plenary Assemblies that approved and amended the text. That line is the source for first-approval years, because the ITU landing pages show only the in-force edition date.
- The ETHW oral-history transcripts contain transcription errors (for example "Kodak" for Codex and "telescope" for trellis in the Ungerboeck transcript). No damaged sentence is quoted without `[sic]`.

## 1. 1200 bit/s: VA3400, Bell 212A, V.22, Smartmodem 1200

### 1. Vadic Corp. (Mountain View, California) announced the VA3400 in December 1972: a 1,200 bit/s modem that ran full-duplex on one 2-wire dial-up line. Introduction was set for February 1973 and first deliveries for the first quarter of 1973. The price was $600.
- **Source:** Ronald A. Frank, "Full-Duplex 1,200 Bit/Sec Modem Can Halve Line Cost", Computerworld, 20 Dec. 1972, pp. 1 and 4. https://archive.org/details/computerworld651unse
- **Excerpt A:** "The modem, scheduled for first deliveries in the first quarter of 1973, performs 103-compatible handshaking at 202 data rates."
- **Excerpt B:** "The $600 VA3400 provides terminal-controlled disconnect through the Data Terminal Ready signal."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (contemporary news report). See Disputed D1 for the other dates in circulation.

### 2. The problem the VA3400 solved: 1,200 bit/s on a dial-up line was half-duplex (Bell 202 type) or needed four wires. Full duplex on two wires removed the line-turnaround delay and halved line cost.
- **Source:** Computerworld, 20 Dec. 1972 (as claim 1).
- **Excerpt:** "[A] full-duplex modem would allow us to perform the same operation on a 2-wire circuit and save one wire-pair or 50% of our present line costs" (the first word is OCR-damaged)
- **Confidence:** HIGH (a user quoted in the news report)

### 3. The first VA3400 units shipped in May 1973. The designers say the product was not robust until a redesigned version in about 1975.
- **Source A:** John Bingham, interviewed by James L. Pelkey, 23 March 1988, Computer History Museum ref. X5671.2010, transcript p. 5. https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2017/09/102738563-05-01-acc.pdf
- **Excerpt A:** "We shipped the first ones in May of 1973, but it was arguable that they were not really completed until long after that"
- **Source B:** Kim Maxwell, interviewed by James L. Pelkey, 19 July 1988, Computer History Museum. https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2017/09/102740207-05-01-acc.pdf
- **Excerpt B:** "We introduced it. We shipped 20. We may have shipped a couple hundred in the first year, but through 1976, a good deal of our business was still in 202s and 103s."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (two participants, recorded 15 years later; they agree with the 1972 news report)

### 4. Credit for the VA3400: John Bingham (ex-GTE Lenkurt, Fairchild, Memorex; joined Vadic June 1972) supplied the theory and the filter design. Bingham says "Ken" defined the product as a "2 x 12" modem. Ted Saunders contributed the active RC filter circuits. Kim Maxwell was Vadic's president from 1972 to 1974.
- **Source:** Bingham interview (as claim 3), transcript pp. 4–5.
- **Excerpt:** "So I provided the technical idea --the theoretical idea -- Ken came up with the product, defined the product that we ought to design"
- **Confidence:** HIGH for Bingham and Saunders. MEDIUM for "Ken": the transcript does not give a surname at this point (the only Ken it names elsewhere is Ken Krechmer). See Disputed D3.

### 5. The hard part of the VA3400 was filtering. On a dial-up line the modem's own reflected transmit signal can be far stronger than the received signal, so about 60–65 dB of filtering was needed to separate the two frequency bands.
- **Source:** Bingham interview (as claim 3), transcript p. 5.
- **Excerpt:** "you finish up having to do something like 60 or 65 dB of filtering to extract the little tiny receive signal from the very large reflected transmit signal."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 6. The VA3400 had no scrambler and no Bell 103 (300 bit/s) mode. AT&T's later 212A had both. Bingham says ICs for a scrambler were not available at design time and that compatibility with the installed base prevented adding one later.
- **Source:** Bingham interview (as claim 3), transcript pp. 6–7.
- **Excerpt:** "the idea of putting in a scrambler, which we regretted to the end of the 3400 time that we had not done, but we just couldn't because there weren't any chips available that wouldn't get awfully hot."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 7. In September 1976 the Bell System still had no 2-wire full-duplex 1,200 bit/s modem. Computerworld credited Vadic with the first one.
- **Source:** Ronald A. Frank, "Bell Low-Speed Modems Not Pacesetters", Computerworld, 27 Sept. 1976, pp. 1 and 8. https://archive.org/details/sim_computerworld_1976-09-27_10_39
- **Excerpt:** "This feature, introduced in the Vadic VA3400, is still not available from the Bell System today."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 8. AT&T introduced the Bell 212A (also called "Dataphone 300/1200") at the Info '76 conference in Chicago in the week of 8 November 1976. It was AT&T's first 2-wire full-duplex 1,200 bit/s data set. It also worked at up to 300 bit/s with Bell 100-series data sets.
- **Source A:** Ronald A. Frank, "AT&T Setting Stage at Info '76 For Debut of Modems, Terminal", Computerworld, 8 Nov. 1976, p. 1. https://archive.org/details/sim_computerworld_1976-11-08_10_45
- **Excerpt A:** "AT&T will show its full-duplex 1,200 bit/sec data set, the Model 212A. Full-duplex transmission of data at 1,200 bit/sec was pioneered by Vadic Corp. in 1973"
- **Source B:** Computerworld, 15 Nov. 1976 (Info '76 report). https://archive.org/details/sim_computerworld_1976-11-15_10_46
- **Excerpt B:** "AT&T also introduced its first two-wire, full-duplex 1,200 bit/sec modem called the 212A. The data set operates at speeds up to 300 bit/sec or 1,200 bit/sec."
- **Confidence:** HIGH. See Disputed D2 for the "September 1976" date.

### 9. The 212A was to rent for about $40 per month. The first tariffs were to be filed after January 1977, with general availability in the first quarter of 1977.
- **Source:** Computerworld, 15 Nov. 1976 (as claim 8).
- **Excerpt:** "The data set will cost about $40/mo and a two-tier pricing plan will be announced later."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (announced price and schedule; actual tariff dates not checked)

### 10. The 212A was not compatible with the VA3400 or with the 1976 Universal Data Systems full-duplex modem. Bell had evaluated the Vadic modem for possible Bell System use and then built its own design at Bell Labs.
- **Source:** Editorial, "Washington Simmering, Bell Cooking", Computerworld, 29 Nov. 1976. https://archive.org/details/sim_computerworld_1976-11-29_10_48
- **Excerpt:** "But the 212A uses different technical standards and is therefore not compatible with either of the two independent modems."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 11. Vadic answered with the VA3467 "triple modem". A built-in microprocessor recognized whether the caller was a VA3400, a 212A or a 103 and answered in that mode. It was advertised in January 1978 at "$850 or less".
- **Source:** Vadic Corporation advertisement, Computerworld, 16 Jan. 1978. https://archive.org/details/computerworld1211unse_02
- **Excerpt:** "[...] new VA3467 automatically recognizes the calling modem, be it a VA3400, a 212A or a 103, and responds accordingly."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (period advertising; the price is the advertised price)

### 12. Racal Electronics bought Vadic in 1978 for $20 million. The name "Racal-Vadic" therefore does not apply to the company that launched the VA3400 in 1972–73.
- **Source:** James L. Pelkey, summary of the Kim Maxwell interview, "History of Computer Communications" (Computer History Museum companion site). https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/interviews/Kim-Maxwell/
- **Excerpt:** "Internal strife between the Board and management resulted in the sale to Racal Electronics in 1978 for $20M, in a year when Vadic's sales grew to $10M."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (interviewer's summary; see Disputed D1 for an error in the companion Bingham summary)

### 13. CCITT Recommendation V.22 was first approved at the Geneva Plenary Assembly in 1980 and amended in 1984 and 1988.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22 (11/88, Blue Book extract), title block. Landing page: https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.22/en — PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.22-198811-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt:** "(Geneva, 1980; amended at Malaga-Torremolinos, 1984; and at Melbourne, 1988)"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 14. How V.22 works: two frequency-divided channels, differential phase-shift modulation at 600 baud, 2 bits per symbol ("dibits"), and a scrambler.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22 (as claim 13), § 1.1 and § 2.5.2.1.
- **Excerpt A:** "differential phase shift modulation for each channel with synchronous line transmission at 600 bauds (nominal)"
- **Excerpt B:** "The data stream to be transmitted shall be divided into groups of 2 consecutive bits (dibits). Each dibit shall be encoded as a phase change relative to the phase of the preceding signal element"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 15. V.22 carriers are 1200 Hz (low channel) and 2400 Hz (high channel). An 1800 Hz guard tone goes with the high channel; a 550 Hz guard tone is a national option.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22 (as claim 13), § 2.1.
- **Excerpt:** "The carrier frequencies shall be 1200 ± 0.5 Hz for the low channel and 2400 ± 1 Hz for the high channel."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 16. The V.22 scrambler is self-synchronizing with generating polynomial 1 + x^-14 + x^-17.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22 (as claim 13), § 5.1.
- **Excerpt:** "A self synchronizing scrambler having the generating polynomial 1 ⊕ x-14⊕ x-17 shall be included in the modem transmitter."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (exponents are superscripts in the original; the PDF text layer flattens them)

### 17. What a scrambler is for, in a designer's words: it randomizes the user's data so that steady or repeating patterns do not upset the receiver's carrier-recovery circuits. The cost is error multiplication by about three.
- **Source:** Bingham interview (as claim 3), transcript p. 6.
- **Excerpt:** "A scrambler is a device that randomizes the customers' data, in order to make sure that certain critical circuits, generally in the receiver, don't get hung up on certain steady, repeated patterns."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 18. In 1986 Computerworld described three incompatible 1,200 bit/s schemes: AT&T 212A (dominant in the U.S.), CCITT V.22, and Racal-Vadic's VA3400.
- **Source:** "Which standard is standard?" (Product Spotlight: 2,400 Bit/Sec. Modems), Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986, p. 43. https://archive.org/details/computerworld207unse
- **Excerpt:** "Three players enter the game at 1,200 bit/sec.: AT&T, CCITT and Racal-Vadic, Inc. In the U.S., the dominant 1,200 bit/sec. standard is the AT&T 212A."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (trade press; author not shown in the OCR). See Disputed D8 on how incompatible V.22 and 212A really were.

### 19. The Hayes Smartmodem 1200 was on sale by December 1982. Hayes advertised it as four times faster than a 300 bit/s modem and as two modems in one (Bell 103 type at up to 300 bit/s, plus 1200 bit/s). Dealers in the same issue listed it at $540 to $589.
- **Source:** Hayes Microcomputer Products advertisement and dealer price lists, PC Magazine, Dec. 1982. https://archive.org/details/PC-Mag-1982-12
- **Excerpt:** "Four times faster than any 300 bps modem, to be precise."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that it was on the market by December 1982. The "two modems in one" and "Bell 103 type modems at up to 300 bps" wording is in the ad, but the OCR interleaves two columns there, so it is paraphrased here, not quoted. The announcement date and venue are not verified (see Could not verify).

### 20. The list price of the standalone Smartmodem 1200 was $699. The internal board version (1200B) listed at $599 with Smartcom II software.
- **Source:** PC Magazine, 21 Aug. 1984 (modem article). https://archive.org/details/PC-Mag-1984-08-21
- **Excerpt:** "The standalone version of the Hayes Smartmodem 1200 lists for $699. The board version lists for $599."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (1984 statement of list price; dealer ads from 1983–84 in InfoWorld, BYTE and PC Magazine also show "$699" as list. The launch-day price is not separately verified.)

## 2. 2400 bit/s: V.22bis

### 21. CCITT Recommendation V.22bis was approved at the Malaga-Torremolinos Plenary Assembly in 1984 and amended at Melbourne in 1988.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22bis (11/88, Blue Book extract), title block. Landing page: https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.22bis/en — PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.22bis-198811-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt:** "(Malaga-Torremolinos, 1984; and al [sic] Melbourne, 1988)"
- **Confidence:** HIGH. See Disputed D4: trade press dates the proposal to late 1981 and the first products to 1982.

### 22. How V.22bis works: the same two carriers and the same 600 baud as V.22, but quadrature amplitude modulation carrying 4 bits per symbol ("quadbits"), which gives 16 signal points. An adaptive equalizer is mandatory. It falls back to V.22 at 1200 bit/s.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22bis (as claim 21), § 1 and § 2.
- **Excerpt A:** "quadrature amplitude modulation for each channel with synchronous line transmission at 600 baud (nominal)"
- **Excerpt B:** "The data stream to be transmitted shall be divided into groups of 4 consecutive bits (quadbits)."
- **Confidence:** HIGH. "16 points" is my arithmetic from 4 bits per symbol; the Recommendation's handshake text speaks of the receiver making "16-way decisions".

### 23. The first V.22bis modems came from Concord Data Systems and Racal-Vadic at about $1,700 and sold to the mainframe market. Sales took off after AT&T introduced its 2224 series in summer 1984. PC-market 2400 bit/s modems were announced at fall COMDEX 1984.
- **Source:** "2400-bps Modems" (Communications feature), PC Magazine, 23 July 1985, pp. 131–138. https://archive.org/details/PC-Mag-1985-07-23
- **Excerpt:** "Concord Data Systems (CDS) and Racal-Vadic became the first companies to offer V.22 bis modems. Both modems cost about $1,700, or twice as much as the Hayes Smartmodem 1200."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (trade-press history; byline not visible in the OCR). PC Magazine dates this to 1982; see Disputed D4.

### 24. The Hayes Smartmodem 2400 listed at $899 in July 1985. The U.S. Robotics Courier 2400 was $699 (cut in February 1985) and the Microcom Era 2 2400 was $799.
- **Source:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985 (as claim 23), fact box and text.
- **Excerpt:** "Most of these modems were originally announced with a list price of just under $900. But by February 1985, the competition was already heating up, and U.S. Robotics dropped its price to $699."
- **Confidence:** HIGH for the July 1985 list prices (fact box: "Smartmodem 2400 [...] List Price: $899"). See Disputed D10 for the "$549" figure.

### 25. The Smartmodem 2400 supported Bell 103 (0–300), Bell 212 (1200), V.22 (600 or 1200) and V.22bis (2400).
- **Source:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985 (as claim 23).
- **Excerpt:** "The Smartmodem 2400 is compatible with four modem protocols: Bell 103 at 0-300 bps, Bell 212 at 1200 bps, V.22 at 600 bps or 1200 bps, and V.22 bis at 2400 bps."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (hands-on review)

### 26. Significance: V.22bis was the first dial-up modem standard that North America and the CCITT countries shared. AT&T took part in it and put its own 2,400 bit/s scheme second.
- **Source A:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985 (as claim 23).
- **Excerpt A:** "Since AT&T participated in developing the V.22 bis protocol at the recommendation of the CCITT, the protocol may well become the first worldwide standard."
- **Source B:** Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986 (as claim 18).
- **Excerpt B:** "Much to the relief of most in the industry, vendors have selected just one standard for 2,400 bit/sec. full-duplex transmission — the CCITT's V.22 bis."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (two contemporary trade-press assessments)

### 27. Before V.22bis the split was complete at low speed: Bell 103 in North America against V.21 in Europe, and Bell 212A against V.22.
- **Source:** Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986 (as claim 18).
- **Excerpt:** "So incompatible are these two standards that many European countries have outlawed the use of AT&T-standard modems, the tones from which seem to discombobulate European telephone billing systems."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (the "outlawed" statement is the magazine's; no regulation was checked)

### 28. The shared standard was not perfectly clean. V.22bis specifies V.22 as the 1200 bit/s fallback, but some North American vendors used 212A for fallback. The Smartmodem 2400 needed a command to switch between Bell and CCITT modes.
- **Source A:** Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986 (as claim 18).
- **Excerpt A:** "Naturally, especially in North America, some vendors have decided to plug in the 212A scheme for fallback instead."
- **Source B:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985 (as claim 23).
- **Excerpt B:** "If set for Bell protocols, the modem will not recognize 2400 bps. If set for CCITT protocols, it will not recognize 300 bps."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

## 3. Error correction and compression: XMODEM, MNP, V.42, V.42bis

### 29. XMODEM began as the file-transfer method in Ward Christensen's MODEM.ASM program, written in August 1977 and placed in the public domain. The name comes from Keith Petersen's XMODEM.ASM adaptation.
- **Source:** Chuck Forsberg (ed.), "XMODEM/YMODEM Protocol Reference", formatted 14 Oct. 1988, § 1.1 and § 3.1 (reproduces a CompuServe message from Ward Christensen dated 25 Apr. 1985). http://timeline.textfiles.com/1988/10/14/1/FILES/ymodem.txt
- **Excerpt A:** "XMODEM refers to the file transfer etiquette introduced by Ward Christensen's 1977 MODEM.ASM program. The name XMODEM comes from Keith Petersen's XMODEM.ASM program"
- **Excerpt B:** "ONLY the fact that it was done in 8/77, and that I put it in the public domain immediately, made it become the standard that it is."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (the author's own statement, as reproduced in the standard reference document)

### 30. James M. Dow left a sales job at Data General in fall 1980 to found Microcom. The first product (software) came out in summer 1981. The first hardware product came out around December 1982 – January 1983.
- **Source:** James Dow, interviewed by James L. Pelkey, 25 Feb. 1988, Computer History Museum, transcript pp. 2–3. https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2020/04/102792045-05-01-acc.pdf
- **Excerpt:** "I formed in 1980. The first product came out in the summer of '81. The first hardware-based product came out about a year and a half after that"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 31. Dow credits the design of MNP to Ed Belove (ex-Data General) and Greg Pearson (ex-DEC and Bolt, Beranek and Newman).
- **Source:** Dow interview (as claim 30), transcript p. 2.
- **Excerpt:** "I added a guy by the name of Ed Belove, who was the first real development engineer in the company, and Ed hired a guy by the name of Greg Pearson, and between Ed and Greg Pearson, they developed MNP."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 32. The problem MNP solved: personal-computer users moved files, and files had to arrive intact. Terminal users had not needed error control. MNP started as file-transfer software on the PC and then moved into the modem.
- **Source A:** Dow interview (as claim 30), transcript p. 4.
- **Excerpt A:** "Terminals don't need error control, because they don't send files."
- **Source B:** Pelkey, summary of the Dow interview. https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/interviews/James-Dow/
- **Excerpt B:** "Microcom's response was to start selling their own modems and to include MNP in the modem itself."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (A), MEDIUM (B, interviewer's summary)

### 33. Microcom made MNP public in July 1983 and licensed it. By early 1988 Dow claimed that every U.S. modem maker except Hayes sold an MNP modem.
- **Source:** Dow interview (as claim 30), transcript p. 4.
- **Excerpt:** "Virtually everybody who is in the modem industry in the United States today, with the exception of Hayes, sells an MNP modem, because they can't compete without it."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that Dow said it; the market claim is his.

### 34. MNP classes as Microcom advertised them in 1988: Class 4 "Adaptive Packet Assembly" sent larger packets on clean lines and smaller ones on noisy lines (up to 122% of rated speed). Class 5 added data compression (overall factor 1.6 to 1).
- **Source:** Microcom advertisement, PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988 (immediately before the article that opens on p. 105; the ad page has no folio in the OCR). https://archive.org/details/PC-Mag-1988-04-26
- **Excerpt:** "MNP Class 4's Adaptive Packet Assembly generates larger packets over clean lines, for increased throughput and reduced protocol overhead."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that these were Microcom's claims (period advertising). The performance figures are the vendor's. Definitions of Classes 1–3 are not verified (see Could not verify).

### 35. By 1992 PC Magazine described MNP 2, 3 and 4 as public-domain error control and MNP 5 as proprietary, licensed compression.
- **Source:** "Modem Standards: MNP, CCITT, and You" (sidebar and chart), PC Magazine, 17 March 1992, p. 307. https://archive.org/details/PC-Mag-1992-03-17
- **Excerpt:** "Microcom's standards 5 and up are proprietary and thus must be licensed by other modem vendors."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (the chart text is jumbled in the OCR; the sentence quoted is intact)

### 36. CCITT Recommendation V.42 was approved at Melbourne in 1988. It defines an HDLC-based protocol, LAPM (Link Access Procedure for Modems), and an "alternative procedure" in Annex A. A compliant modem must implement both; two V.42 modems use LAPM with each other.
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.42 (11/88, Blue Book re-edition), title block and § 1.1. Landing page: https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.42/en — PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.42-198811-S!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt:** "Compliance with this Recommendation requires implementation of both protocols. However, unless otherwise specified by user options, two V.42 DCEs will communicate using LAPM."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 37. Annex A of V.42 is MNP error control (Classes 2–4). The Recommendation itself does not name Microcom; it says only that modems using the Annex A protocol were already in service and that the CCITT would not develop Annex A further.
- **Source A:** CCITT Rec. V.42 (as claim 36), § 1.1.
- **Excerpt A:** "It is not intended to further enhance the protocol defined in Annex A."
- **Source B:** PC Magazine, 17 March 1992 (as claim 35), chart entry for V.42.
- **Excerpt B:** "A protocol that incorporates a fallback to MNP 2, 3, and 4 if LAPM, the primary protocol, is not available."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (A); MEDIUM for the identification of Annex A with MNP 2–4 (B)

### 38. The V.42 outcome was a compromise between an "LAP camp" and an "MNP camp" in the CCITT. Dow's account, two months before the 1988 plenary: Britain alone among countries pushed LAP, with Hayes and AT&T; several European administrations backed MNP; the U.S. had no position.
- **Source:** Dow interview (as claim 30), transcript pp. 4–5.
- **Excerpt:** "What we agreed to was put a standard in place and then have LAP and MNP."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that this is Dow's account. It is one party's version; no CCITT record of the debate was read.

### 39. CCITT Recommendation V.42bis (data compression) was prepared by Study Group XVII and approved on 31 January 1990. It needs an error-corrected link underneath (V.42 LAPM) and switches automatically to a transparent mode when the data will not compress.
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.42bis (01/90), Foreword and § 1. Landing page: https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.42bis/en — PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.42bis-199001-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt:** "Recommendation V.42 bis was prepared by Study Group XVII and was approved under the Resolution No. 2 procedure on the 31st of January 1990."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 40. The V.42bis algorithm is "BTLZ" (British Telecom Lempel-Ziv). British Telecom submitted it to Study Group XVII in October 1988. The study group tested it against three proprietary schemes (ACT CommPressor, Hayes Adaptive Data Compression, Microcom MNP) and judged it better. Alan Clark of British Telecom's research laboratories did the work that made it cheap to implement.
- **Source:** John Cox, "Proposed V.42bis standard heralds speedier modems", Network World, 22 May 1989, p. 17. https://archive.org/details/networkworld620unse
- **Excerpt:** "Last October, the British firm submitted what it called the British Telecom Lempel-Ziv (BTLZ) algorithm to SG17, which tested the algorithm and the three others cited above on a wide range of different data files."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (contemporary trade press quoting study-group members Ken Krechmer and John Copeland; the OCR renders "SG17" as "SGI 7"). See Disputed D9 for an error in the same article.

### 41. The transparent-mode idea (turn compression off for uncompressible data such as encrypted files) is attributed to John Copeland of Hayes, a member of the study group.
- **Source:** Network World, 22 May 1989 (as claim 40).
- **Excerpt:** "Copeland proposed a change that lets BTLZ turn itself off when it detects uncompressable data, such as encrypted data, and then back on again when regular data appears."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 42. Throughput claims for V.42bis: study-group members said "two to four times". Modem vendors rounded this up to "4:1" and multiplied the line rate (14,400 × 4 = 57,600 bit/s).
- **Source A:** Network World, 22 May 1989 (as claim 40).
- **Excerpt A:** "The proposed standard, called V.42bis, will boost the throughput of full-duplex modems by two to four times in most cases"
- **Source B:** Supra Corporation press release, January 1992, reproduced in ST Report No. 8.03, 17 Jan. 1992. https://archive.org/details/st-report-803
- **Excerpt B:** "When using V.42bis (4:1 compression), the SupraFAXModem V.32bis provides up to 57,600 bps throughput"
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (A), HIGH that the vendor made the claim (B). The Recommendation text does not state a compression ratio.

## 4. Proprietary 9600-class stopgaps before affordable V.32

### 43. The first commercial 9600 bit/s modem was a leased-line product: the Codex AE-96 (single-sideband, digital adaptive equalizer, designed by Jerry Holsinger). First shipments were about 1969 and the price was $23,000.

## 5. 9600 bit/s: V.32 and the three enabling technologies

### 58. CCITT Recommendation V.32 was approved at the Malaga-Torremolinos Plenary Assembly in 1984 and amended at Melbourne in 1988 (a further edition is dated 03/93).
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.32 (11/88, Blue Book re-edition), title block. Landing page: https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.32/en — PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.32-198811-S!!PDF-E&type=items

- **Confidence:** HIGH. See Disputed D5 for "1986".

### 59. V.32 in one line each: full duplex on two wires; both directions use the same band and are separated by echo cancellation; QAM at 2400 baud; carrier 1800 Hz; 9600 and 4800 bit/s.
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.32 (as claim 58), § 1 and § 2.1.
- **Excerpt A:** "Channel separation by echo cancellation techniques."

- **Excerpt C:** "The carrier frequency is to be 1800 ± 1 Hz."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 60. V.32 offers two ways to send 9600 bit/s: an uncoded 16-point constellation, or trellis coding with 32 points. Every 9600 bit/s V.32 modem must be able to interwork using the 16-point scheme.
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.32 (as claim 58), § 1 e).
- **Excerpt:** "At 9600 bit/s, two alternative modulation schemes, one using 16 carrier states and one using trellis coding with 32 carrier states, are provided for in this Recommendation."

- **Confidence:** HIGH

### (a) Adaptive equalization

### 62. Robert W. Lucky of Bell Labs published "Automatic Equalization for Digital Communication" in the Bell System Technical Journal, April 1965 (manuscript received 24 November 1964). It describes a tapped delay line (transversal filter) whose tap gains are set automatically by a steepest-descent method during a training period of test pulses.
- **Source:** R. W. Lucky, BSTJ vol. 44 no. 4, April 1965, pp. 547 ff. https://archive.org/details/bstj44-4-547
- **Excerpt:** "An easily implemented system for automatic equalization is described which makes use of a steepest-descent technique of minimization. The equalizer is automatically set prior to data transmission in a training period during which a series of test pulses is transmitted."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (the paper itself)

### 63. Lucky's second paper, February 1966 (manuscript received 14 October 1965), made the equalizer *adaptive*: it keeps adjusting during data transmission, without test signals, as the channel changes.
- **Source:** R. W. Lucky, "Techniques for Adaptive Equalization of Digital Communication Systems", BSTJ vol. 45 no. 2, Feb. 1966, pp. 255 ff. https://archive.org/details/bstj45-2-255
- **Excerpt:** "The present paper extends the utility of this automatic equalization system by permitting it to change settings during the data transmission period in response to changes in transmission channel characteristics."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 64. Lucky dates his adaptive-equalizer work to 1965–66 and says it raised the top modem speed from 2400 to 9600 bit/s.
- **Source:** Robert Lucky, oral history conducted by David Hochfelder, IEEE History Center, 10 Sept. 1999 (Interview #361). https://ethw.org/Oral-History:Robert_Lucky
- **Excerpt:** "When I joined Bell Labs in 1961 the highest modem speeds were 2400 bits per second, and when I invented the adaptive equalizer it enabled a speed of 9600 bits per second."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that Lucky says so. See Disputed D11 for "1964".

### 65. The problem it solved: a pulse sent over a telephone channel comes out smeared and interferes with neighbouring pulses (intersymbol interference). An adaptive filter learns the channel and corrects for it.
- **Source:** Lucky oral history (as claim 64).
- **Excerpt:** "Unless you pass this through some kind of adaptive filter that learns the channel characteristics and corrects for them you are limiting speed – because you have the self-noise of pulses interfering with each other."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 66. The first Bell Labs adaptive equalizers were built from mechanical relays on a rack about six feet high, by a development group headed by Floyd Becker.
- **Source:** Lucky oral history (as claim 64).
- **Excerpt:** "We made those first adaptive equalizers out of relays. We had hundreds of relays on a big rack. Digital logic was not cheap."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (the transcript says both "hundreds of relays" and "about a hundred relays" within a few lines)

### 67. Lucky's own criticism of his work: he favoured the "zero-forcing" criterion and gave too little weight to the mean-square-error criterion, which became the usual one; and he did not press decision-directed adaptation hard enough in his patents.
- **Source:** Lucky oral history (as claim 64).
- **Excerpt:** "However in my exuberance about the mathematics of this I gave little credence to mean square error criteria."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 68. By 1984 an adaptive equalizer was a required part even of the 2400 bit/s standard (V.22bis). The 1976 leased-line 9600 bit/s standard V.29 also lists one as a principal characteristic.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.22bis (as claim 21), § 1 e); ITU-T Rec. V.29 (11/88), § 1 d). https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.29/en

- **Confidence:** HIGH

### (b) Echo cancellation

### 69. The echo canceller was introduced by M. M. Sondhi and A. J. Presti of Bell Labs in a BSTJ Brief, December 1966 (manuscript received 14 October 1966). They named it "echo canceller" and credited the underlying idea to J. L. Kelly, Jr. and B. F. Logan.
- **Source:** M. M. Sondhi and A. J. Presti, "A Self-Adaptive Echo Canceller", BSTJ vol. 45 no. 10, Dec. 1966, pp. 1851–1854. https://archive.org/details/bstj45-10-1851
- **Excerpt:** "It is based upon an idea originally proposed by J. L. Kelly, Jr. and B. F. Logan, and incorporates modifications which simplify and improve the implementation and performance."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (the paper itself)

### 70. Sondhi's full paper followed in March 1967. The motive was echo on long-delay circuits such as satellite links, where voice-switched echo suppressors chop the conversation.
- **Source:** M. M. Sondhi, "An Adaptive Echo Canceller", BSTJ vol. 46 no. 3, March 1967, pp. 497 ff. https://archive.org/details/bstj46-3-497
- **Excerpt:** "A replica of the echo is synthesized and subtracted from the return signal."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 71. Echo cancellation for *data* (full-duplex modems on two wires) has this publication trail: Koll and Weinstein (1973); Mueller (1976); Weinstein (1977); Falconer and Mueller (1979). In the 1979 paper the canceller is a "data-driven transversal filter": its input is the modem's own transmitted symbols.
- **Source:** D. D. Falconer and K. H. Mueller, "Adaptive Echo Cancellation/AGC Structures for Two-Wire, Full-Duplex Data Transmission", BSTJ vol. 58 no. 7, Sept. 1979, pp. 1593 ff., text and reference list. https://archive.org/details/bstj58-7-1593

## 7. "Baud" versus "bit/s"

### 94. The baud was defined at the first meeting of the CCIT (Comité consultatif international des communications télégraphiques) in Berlin, November 1926. The committee gave the opinion that a transmission speed of one elementary interval per second be equal to 1 baud, "to honour the memory of the great telegraphist Emile Baudot".
- **Source:** *Documents de la première réunion du Comité consultatif international des communications télégraphiques à Berlin, novembre 1926*, Tome I, Bureau international de l'Union télégraphique, Berne, March 1927, pp. 55–56 (ITU Library & Archives scan). https://search.itu.int/history/HistoryDigitalCollectionDocLibrary/4.242.43.fr.200.pdf
- **Excerpt:** "que la vitesse de transfert d'un intervalle par seconde soit égale à 1 baud, pour honorer la mémoire du grand télégraphiste Emile Baudot."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (primary document). Note the body: the telegraph committee CCIT, not the later CCITT. See Disputed D13.

### 95. The 1926 definition already makes baud a count of signalling intervals: speed is "the inverse of the value of the elementary interval expressed in seconds". The annex adds that the number of cycles per second is half the number of bauds.
- **Source:** CCIT Berlin 1926, Tome I (as claim 94), pp. 55–56.
- **Excerpt:** "que la vitesse de transfert soit exprimée, pour les systèmes de la Ie classe, par l'inverse de la valeur de l'intervalle élémentaire exprimée en secondes"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 96. A clean modern definition: baud is a unit of modulation rate; one baud is one unit interval per second. If each interval can take one of n states, bit rate = baud rate × log2 n. Using "baud" to mean bit/s is deprecated.
- **Source:** ATIS Telecom Glossary, "baud (Bd)". https://glossary.atis.org/glossary/baud-bd/
- **Excerpt A:** "One baud corresponds to a rate of one unit interval per second, where the modulation rate is expressed as the reciprocal of the duration in seconds of the shortest unit interval."
- **Excerpt B:** "Baud is sometimes used as a synonym for bit-per-second. This usage is deprecated."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (standards-body glossary)

### 97. Worked examples straight from the Recommendations: V.22 = 600 baud × 2 bits = 1200 bit/s; V.22bis = 600 baud × 4 bits = 2400 bit/s; V.32 = 2400 baud × 4 data bits = 9600 bit/s; V.32bis = 2400 symbols/s × 6 data bits = 14,400 bit/s. So a "9600 baud modem" never existed on a phone line in this period; no modem here ran faster than 2400 baud.
- **Source:** ITU texts cited in claims 14, 22, 59–61, 83–84.
- **Excerpt:** see those claims.
- **Confidence:** HIGH for the figures; the last sentence is my inference from them and covers only the standards in this sheet.

### 98. The trade press knew the difference in 1988 and explained why people confused the two.
- **Source:** PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988 (as claim 45), glossary by Howard Marks.
- **Excerpt:** "While this term is often used to mean bits per second (bps), each transition may represent more than 1 bit. The misuse arises because on a standard RS-232 interface, each transition represents one bit."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 99. The CCITT itself changed wording between 1984/88 and 1991: V.32 says "2400 bauds"; V.32bis says "2400 symbols/s".
- **Source:** CCITT Rec. V.32 § 1 c) (claim 59) and CCITT Rec. V.32bis § 1 c) (claim 83).
- **Excerpt:** "synchronous line transmission at 2400 bauds" (V.32); "synchronous line transmission at 2400 symbols/s" (V.32bis)
- **Confidence:** HIGH

## Disputed / uncertain

### D1. When was the VA3400 "introduced"?
All of these come from sources read for this sheet.
- **December 1972 (announced); February 1973 (planned introduction); Q1 1973 (planned first deliveries).** Computerworld, 20 Dec. 1972 (claim 1).
- **"Early in 1973" (began to install).** Computerworld, 27 Sept. 1976: "Early in 1973, Vadic Corp. began to install a modem that allowed full-duplex operation over two-wire facilities at 1,200 bit/sec."
- **1973.** Computerworld, 8 Nov. 1976 ("pioneered by Vadic Corp. in 1973"); Vadic ad, Computerworld, 16 Jan. 1978 ("Vadic invented the 1200 bps full duplex modem (the VA3400) way back in 1973").
- **May 1973 (first shipments).** Bingham and Maxwell interviews (claim 3). Maxwell also says: "we announced a product, unfortunately in the winter of 1972, early 1973".
- **1974.** Computerworld editorial, 29 Nov. 1976: "follows the technology pioneered by Vadic Corp. in 1974".
- **May 1975.** Pelkey's summary page for the Bingham interview: "The team released the VA3400 in May 1975." https://historyofcomputercommunications.info/interviews/John-Bingham/ — This contradicts the transcript the page links to ("May of 1973"; a redesigned version "came out in '75").
- **Assessment:** announced December 1972; first shipped May 1973; a robust redesign about 1975. "1974" and "May 1975" look like errors. Wikipedia-derived pages give "December 1972" or "early 1973"; those were used only as leads.

### D2. When was the Bell 212A introduced?
- **Week of 8 November 1976, at Info '76 in Chicago.** Computerworld, 8 and 15 Nov. 1976 (claim 8). Availability "from most Bell companies by the first quarter of 1977".
- **September 1976.** Bingham: "The date I've got in mind is September of '76, but that was the date that was written on their compatibility specs. Whether the modem came out a few months before or after that I don't remember." Pelkey's summaries repeat "September 1976".
- **Assessment:** the technical reference is probably dated September 1976; the public introduction was November 1976. The Bell technical reference itself was not read.

### D3. Who was "first" with 2-wire full-duplex 1200 bit/s, and who designed it?
- Computerworld (1976, three articles) credits Vadic. It also notes that Universal Data Systems introduced a similar modem "earlier this year" (1976), before Bell.
- Vadic's patent was lost in litigation with UDS because Vadic filed more than a year after it began marketing (Bingham and Maxwell interviews). No court record was read.
- Bingham says he supplied the theory and "Ken" defined the product. Pelkey's Maxwell summary says "Bingham was a key hire who, with Maxwell and others, innovated the first full-duplex 1200 bps modem". Maxwell's own account has him co-designing the asynchronous interface. The surname of "Ken" at the key passage is not stated; Ken Krechmer is the only Ken named in the Bingham transcript, and Pelkey's summary says Krechmer joined Vadic in 1973, which, if right, is after the product was defined; yet Bingham places "Ken" at Vadic at his first meeting there in 1972. Treat the "Ken" attribution as unresolved.

### D4. Is V.22bis 1984 or 1982?
- **1984:** ITU text, "(Malaga-Torremolinos, 1984 [...])" (claim 21). This is the Plenary Assembly approval.
- **Late 1981 / 1982:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985: "it was proposed in late 1981" and "In 1982, shortly after the V.22 bis protocol was approved as a CCITT recommendation, Concord Data Systems (CDS) and Racal-Vadic became the first companies to offer V.22 bis modems." Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986: "Proposed late in 1981".
- **Assessment:** products shipped on a study-group draft before the 1984 plenary. No CCITT study-group document from 1981–82 was read, so "approved in 1982" is unverified.

### D5. Is V.32 1984 or 1986?
- **1984:** ITU text (claim 58); Forney et al. 1996 ("the V.32 9.6 kb/s modem standard (1984)"); PC Magazine 1991 ("adopted in 1984").
- **1986:** Costello and Forney 2006: "The first international standard to use coding was the V.32 standard (1986)".
- **1982:** Forney oral history says TCM "began to be considered for the V32 modem standard in 1982".
- **Assessment:** 1984 is the approval year. "1986" may reflect when products appeared, or may be a slip; the source does not explain it.

### D6. Courier HST back channel: 300 or 450 bit/s?
- **300 bit/s:** PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988 (claim 52), in both the product review and the protocol diagram caption.
- **"Four channels":** InfoWorld, 3 Nov. 1986 describes HST as "dividing a telephone line into four channels and sending data on all four channels simultaneously" (de-interleaved from two OCR columns), which does not match the later descriptions.
- **450 bit/s:** in the research brief; not found in any source read. Not verified.
- BYTE, July 1993 lists HST at 16.8 kbit/s by then ("USRobotics HST [...] HST uses two fixed-speed channels and simply changes directions as necessary").

### D7. What was the first 9600 bit/s modem?
- Forney: the Codex AE-96, first shipments about 1969, $23,000 (claim 43).
- PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988: "Codex made the first 9,600-bps modem. It appeared in the early 1980s, sold for about $10,000, and was designed for mainframe communications."
- **Assessment:** the same company, a decade apart. Forney was there; PC Magazine's date is very likely wrong. No Codex product literature was read.

### D8. Could a Bell 212A talk to a V.22 modem?
- Computerworld, 17 Feb. 1986: "V.22 and 212A are not compatible."
- PC Magazine, 23 July 1985: "In principle, it is possible for a Bell 212 modem in this country to communicate with a V.22 modem in Europe. In practice, however, according to people who have tried it, there are often difficulties."
- **Assessment:** the modulation is closely similar; answer tone, guard tone and handshake differ. The Bell 212A technical reference was not read, so the exact differences are not verified here.

### D9. Who invented Lempel-Ziv?
- Network World, 22 May 1989 says: "V.42bis is based on the Lempel-Ziv compression algorithm developed by AT&T Bell Laboratories."
- This attribution is doubtful. No primary source on Lempel and Ziv was read for this sheet, so the correction is left to the writer; do not repeat the Bell Labs attribution without checking.

### D10. Smartmodem 2400 launch price: $899 or $549?
- **$899:** PC Magazine, 23 July 1985 fact box (claim 24); a 1987 InfoWorld review still lists $899 (seen as a search snippet only).
- **$549:** Wikipedia "Hayes Microcomputer Products" and pages derived from it (lead only; no primary source found).
- **Assessment:** use $899 for 1985.

### D11. Lucky's equalizer: 1964 or 1965–66?
- **1965–66:** Lucky's oral history ("I did my work on automatic adaptive equalizers in 1965-66"), and the interview abstract.
- **1964:** IEEE Spectrum obituary, 2022: "In 1964 he invented the automatic adaptive equalizer, which is still used in modems today." https://spectrum.ieee.org/bob-lucky-obituary
- **Reconciliation from the papers:** the first (automatic, training-based) paper was received 24 Nov. 1964 and published April 1965; the second (adaptive during data) was received 14 Oct. 1965 and published Feb. 1966. Both dates are defensible depending on which step is meant.

### D12. How much gain did TCM give?
- Lucky: "about a 6 dB gain". ETHW biography of Ungerboeck: "coding gains of at least 3 dB [...] and gains of up to 6 dB are achieved with more complexity." Forney oral history: "three, three-and-a-half, 4 dB". Forney et al. 1996: "about 3.6 dB" for the V.32 code. Costello and Forney 2006: "a real coding gain of about 3.5 dB".
- **Assessment:** about 3.5 dB in V.32; 6 dB is the upper figure for much more complex codes.

### D13. Which body named the baud, and when?
- The primary document (claim 94) is from the CCIT (telegraph committee), Berlin, November 1926, published March 1927.
- Wikipedia-derived pages say "defined by the CCITT (now the ITU-T) in November 1926". The CCITT did not exist in 1926; this sheet did not verify the CCITT's founding date, so say "CCIT, a predecessor of the CCITT" only after checking that lineage.
- One search-result page claimed the term was "formally adopted in 1932"; not verified and not used.

### D14. MNP 5 compression: how much?
- Microcom ad, 1988: "An overall compression factor of 1.6/1 or 63% is achieved."
- PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988 (TrailBlazer review, about Telebit's scheme): "as much as 1.6 to 1".
- PC Magazine, 17 March 1992: MNP 5 "allows for an average increase in throughput of 40 percent".
- PC Magazine, 26 Apr. 1988: 2,400 bit/s modems with MNP Class 5 "are about 60 percent faster than regular 2,400-bps modems."
