# Fact sheet 03 — 28,800, 33,600 and 56,000 bit/s (V.34, V.34 rev. 1996, x2/K56flex, V.90, V.92)

Research date: 2026-11-20. Every source below was opened and read during this task (direct fetch, Wayback Machine copy, or PDF text extraction). Nothing is filled in from memory; where memory would have been needed, the item is in "Could not verify".

**Confidence tags**

- **HIGH** — primary or authoritative source read directly (ITU text, FCC document, patent, the paper itself, a corporate press release, an oral-history transcript).
- **MEDIUM** — reputable secondary source read directly (contemporary press, trade press, a reprint on a third-party site).
- **LOW** — could not fully verify; the reason is stated.

**Reading notes**

- Excerpts are verbatim and at most 40 words. `[...]` marks an omission, `[sic]` an error in the source.
- The Forney et al. paper was read as a scanned PDF. Its OCR text is damaged in places. Line-break hyphens are removed in excerpts; no damaged passage is quoted.
- Google Patents text is machine-extracted. Footnote markers are omitted from FCC excerpts.
- ITU "landing page" dates and ITU PDF dates disagree for two documents (see Disputed D6).

## 1. The road to 28.8k

### 1. The baseline was V.32bis (14,400 bit/s), approved 22 February 1991.
- **Source:** ITU-T, "V.32bis : A duplex modem operating at data signalling rates of up to 14 400 bit/s [...]", edition page (02/91). https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.32bis-199102-I/en
- **Excerpt:** "Recommendation V.32bis (02/91) Approved in 1991-02-22 Status : In force"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 2. V.34 was nicknamed "V.fast" during development. The goal was at least double V.32bis, and participants first thought 28.8k was a stretch.
- **Source:** Dale Walsh (VP advanced development, U.S. Robotics; member of the committee), "V.34: The next-generation modem", Network World, 7 March 1994. Reprint: https://www.krsaborio.net/internet/research/1994/0307.htm
- **Excerpt:** "At the outset of development, most TSS participants believed 28.8K bit/sec was a stretch for dial-up phone connections."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (third-party reprint of the Network World article; the author was a participant)

### 3. V.fast contributions went first to CCITT Study Group XVII (1992 – January 1993) and then to ITU-T Study Group 14 (September 1993). ITU-T Study Group 14 prepared the final text.
- **Source A:** ITU-T Rec. V.34 (09/94), "Source" paragraph. PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.34-199409-S!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt A:** "ITU-T Recommendation V.34 was prepared by ITU-T Study Group 14 (1993-1996) and was approved under the WTSC Resolution No. 1 procedure on the 20th of September 1994."
- **Source B:** Forney, Brown, Eyuboglu, Moran, "The V.34 High-Speed Modem Standard", IEEE Communications Magazine 34(12), Dec. 1996, pp. 28–33 (DOI 10.1109/35.556483), reference list. Copy read: https://www.audentia-gestion.fr/research.microsoft/v34.pdf
- **Excerpt B:** "R. G. C. Williams, "A Trellis Code for V.fast," CCITT V fast Rapporteur meeting, Bath, U.K, Sept. 1992"
- **Confidence:** HIGH for Study Group 14 and the approval date. MEDIUM for the Study Group XVII detail (reference entries [18], [19], [24] name "SG XVII" for June 1992 and Jan. 1993, but the OCR of those lines is damaged).

### 4. The V.fast schedule slipped by about a year in 1993.
- **Source:** Telecompaper, "V.Fast delays modem standard", 19 Sept. 1993 (teaser paragraph; rest is behind registration). https://www.telecompaper.com/news/v-fast-delays-modem-standard--6665
- **Excerpt:** "The V.Fast standards committee has delayed setting a new standard for next-generation modems until July 1994 at the earliest, one year later than expected. Ratification of the new standard is expected to follow in September 1994."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (short trade-news brief; only the first paragraph is public)

### 5. Rockwell unveiled its pre-standard "V.Fast Class" (V.FC) 28.8k chipsets in August 1993 because the ITU standard was late.
- **Source:** Telecompaper, "Rockwell unveils VFC modem chipsets", 16 Aug. 1993. https://www.telecompaper.com/news/rockwell-unveils-vfc-modem-chipsets--3749
- **Excerpt:** "Rockwell has unveiled the V.Fast Class (VFC) modem chipsets, supporting up to 28.8kbps data rates. The launch has been made due to the International Telecommunications Union (ITU) V.Fast standard not being ratified until June 1994."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 6. V.32terbo was an interim, non-ITU scheme above 14.4k. At least one modem maker dropped it in August 1993 for a Rockwell-based 28.8k design, with Hayes.
- **Source A:** Telecompaper, "Dataflex switches from V.32terbo standard", 3 Aug. 1993. https://www.telecompaper.com/news/dataflex-switches-from-v-32terbo-standard--2827
- **Excerpt A:** "Dataflex and Hayes Microcomputer Products will make V.fast modems which include fax capability using a Rockwell International chipset."
- **Source B:** U.S. Robotics, "Courier V.Everything External Modem: Getting Started", p/n 1.024.492, "FINAL 4/96", Appendix B modulation table. https://support.usr.com/support/s-cour/s-cour-docs/1024492.pdf
- **Excerpt B:** "V.32terbo 21.6/19.2/16.8/14.4/12 Kbps; 9600/7200 bps asynchronous Trellis Coded Modulation (TCM)"
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM for the existence and rates as USR implemented them. LOW for the usual statement that AT&T (Paradyne) originated V.32terbo at 19.2k — seen only in search-result snippets and Wikipedia, not in a source I opened. Source A calls the Rockwell-based design "the official V.fast" [sic]; it was pre-standard.

### 7. Pre-standard 28.8k modems were proprietary and worked only with modems of the same scheme.
- **Source:** Walsh, Network World, 7 March 1994 (as claim 2).
- **Excerpt:** "These early-to-market modems only communicate with other modems from the same vendor or a small group of vendors that use the same proprietary scheme and will likely not comply with the V.34 standard when it comes out."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (the author worked for a V.FC competitor at that time — treat as an interested party)

### 8. By 1996 the press treated V.FC as a dead end for owners whose modems could not be upgraded.
- **Source:** Stephen Manes, "Personal Computers; Of Communications a la Modem", The New York Times, 2 April 1996. Read via http://web.archive.org/web/20150526155702/http://www.nytimes.com/1996/04/02/science/personal-computers-of-communications-a-la-modem.html
- **Excerpt:** "Other so-called "standards," like the briefly popular V.FC, are useless unless you must communicate directly with some hapless soul who bought a V.FC modem that cannot be upgraded to V.34."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 9. ITU-T approved V.34 (title: "up to 28 800 bit/s") on 20 September 1994. V.8 was approved the same day.
- **Source A:** ITU-T, V.34 (09/94) edition page. https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.34-199409-S/en
- **Excerpt A:** "Recommendation V.34 (09/94) Approved in 1994-09-20 Status : Superseded"
- **Source B:** ITU-T, V.8 (09/94) edition page. https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.8-199409-S/en
- **Excerpt B:** "Recommendation V.8 (09/94) Approved in 1994-09-20 Status : Superseded"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 10. The technical work was finished in summer 1994, and many vendors shipped V.34 modems soon after.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996 (as claim 3), p. 28.
- **Excerpt:** "The standard was completed in the summer of 1994"
- **Confidence:** HIGH (authors were Motorola participants in the standard)

## 2. What V.34 added

### 11. The biggest single gain came from using all the bandwidth the line allowed, not a fixed 2400-baud band.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 29.
- **Excerpt:** "single most significant factor contributing to the increased bit rates of V.34 is the use of the maximum possible bandwidth permitted by the channel."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 12. V.34 has six symbol rates: 2400, 2743, 2800, 3000, 3200 and 3429. Three are mandatory (2400, 3000, 3200). Carrier frequency is also selectable.
- **Source A:** ITU-T Rec. V.34 (02/98), clause 5. PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.34-199802-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt A:** "2400, 3000, and 3200 are mandatory; 2743, 2800, and 3429 are optional."
- **Source B:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 29.
- **Excerpt B:** "In V.34, however, the bandwidth and carrier frequency are both adaptive, with a maximum bandwidth of about 3429 Hz."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 13. Line probing: during the handshake each modem sends a comb of tones so the other end can measure the channel, then both pick symbol rate, carrier and power.
- **Source A:** ITU-T Rec. V.34 (02/98), definition 3.5.
- **Excerpt A:** "line probing: A method for determining channel characteristics by sending periodic signals, which are analysed by the modem and used to determine data mode modulation parameters."
- **Source B:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 32.
- **Excerpt B:** "The probing signal consists of a set of tones of equal amplitude spaced 150 Hz apart at frequencies from 150 Hz to 3750 Hz."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 14. Trellis coding moved to four-dimensional codes. The adopted mandatory code is Lee-Fang Wei's 16-state 4-D code. Two optional codes exist: a 32-state code by Williams and a 64-state code by Wei.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 29.
- **Excerpt:** "In particular, the 16-state 4-D trellis code of Wei [4] has an effective coding gain of about 4.2 dB, or 0.6 dB better than the V.32 code, with about the same decoding complexity."
- **Confidence:** HIGH. The paper gives "L.-F. Wei" and "R. G. C. Williams" with no affiliations.

### 15. Shell mapping (constellation shaping, about 0.8 dB) is credited by Forney et al. to "many authors", not to one company.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 30 and references [8]–[12] (Lang & Longstaff 1989; Fortier, Ruiz & Cioffi 1992; Kschischang & Pasupathy 1993; Khandani & Kabal 1993; Laroia, Farvardin & Tretter 1994).
- **Excerpt:** "Shell mapping, which has been developed by many authors [8-12], is an algorithmic method of achieving near-spherical constellation shaping in a high number of dimensions with bounded QAM constellation expansion."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (see Disputed D9 for the single-inventor question)

### 16. Precoding puts the feedback half of a decision-feedback equalizer in the transmitter. The idea is from Tomlinson and Harashima; the V.34 form came from successive proposals (Eyuboglu & Forney; Motorola; General DataComm; Laroia, Tretter & Farvardin; AT&T).
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 30 and references [14]–[22].
- **Excerpt:** "This development was an outstanding example of different contributors building on each others' work to develop a series of improvements that none would likely have arrived at individually."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 17. "Nonlinear encoding" is an option that spreads the outer constellation points to resist PCM quantization noise and nonlinear distortion.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 30 (cites Betts, Calderbank & Laroia 1994).
- **Excerpt:** "V.34 also includes an option called "nonlinear encoding," which is designed to combat PCM quantization noise and nonlinear distortion [13]."
- **Confidence:** HIGH. The word "warping" does not appear in this paper.

### 18. V.34 has an optional 200 bit/s auxiliary channel multiplexed with the main data.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.34 (02/98), definition 3.1.
- **Excerpt:** "auxiliary channel: A 200 bit/s data channel which, along with the primary channel, is multiplexed into the bit stream transmitted by the modem."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 19. V.34 is a "best-effort" design: the two modems agree on bandwidth, bit rate and options at start-up, and can shift rate during the call.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 28.
- **Excerpt:** "it will transmit at as high a bit rate as possible, given the characteristics of the actual connection and the capabilities implemented in a particular modem."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 20. A participant's estimate of the processing load: 1,000–2,000 computations per bit.
- **Source:** Walsh, Network World, 7 March 1994 (as claim 2).
- **Excerpt:** "V.34 modems will be able to process between 1,000 and 2,000 computations for each bit sent and received. Compare that with the 200 to 300 calculations the 9.6K bit/sec modems in Group III facsimile machines perform on each bit."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (a forecast in a trade article, not a measurement)

### 21. V.8 (1994) is the start-up procedure. The answering modem sends ANSam: the normal 2100 Hz answer tone, amplitude-modulated at 15 Hz so that a V.8 caller can tell it from an older modem.
- **Source A:** ITU-T Rec. V.8 (11/2000), clause 7.2. PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.8-200011-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt A:** "Modified answer tone ANSam consists of a sinewave signal at 2100 ± 1 Hz with phase reversals at an interval of 450 ± 25 ms, amplitude-modulated by a sinewave at 15 ± 0.1 Hz."
- **Source B:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 31.
- **Excerpt B:** "the normal 2100 Hz answer tone is modulated with a distinctive 15 Hz sine wave with a 20 percent modulation index."
- **Confidence:** HIGH. I read the 2000 edition of V.8, not the 1994 text; the 1994 edition page confirms only title and date.

### 22. V.8 signals CI, CM and JM are 300 bit/s V.21 messages. CI states the call function, CM lists the caller's modulation modes, JM returns the modes both ends share.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.8 (11/2000), definitions 3.1, 3.4, 3.6.
- **Excerpt:** "joint menu signal (JM): A signal (see 7.4) transmitted from the answer DCE primarily to indicate modulation modes available jointly in the call and answer DCEs."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (2000 edition)

### 23. V.8bis was approved 16 August 1996. It lets a call start as voice and then switch to data or video.
- **Source A:** ITU-T, V.8bis (08/96) edition page. https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.8bis-199608-S/en
- **Excerpt A:** "Recommendation V.8bis (08/96) Approved in 1996-08-16 Status : Superseded"
- **Source B:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 32.
- **Excerpt B:** "allowing a call to start in the regular voice mode and then smoothly transition into a voice-and-data or video mode."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

## 3. The Shannon limit

### 24. Shannon's 1948 Theorem 17 gives the capacity of a band-limited channel with white noise: C = W log2((P+N)/N).
- **Source:** C. E. Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", Bell System Technical Journal 27, July and Oct. 1948 (reprint with corrections). https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf
- **Excerpt:** "The capacity of a channel of band W perturbed by white thermal noise power N when the average transmitter power is limited to P is given by"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 25. ESTIMATE: Bell Labs authors put the "conventional Shannon limit" of the phone channel at about 36 kbit/s. They treat PCM quantization noise as the noise term (SNR 33–39 dB, bandwidth 3–3.5 kHz).
- **Source:** Ayanoglu, Dagdeviren, Golden, Mazo, "An Equalizer Design Technique for the PCM Modem: A New Modem for the Digital Public Switched Network", IEEE Trans. Communications 46(6), June 1998, pp. 763–774. https://newport.eecs.uci.edu/~ayanoglu/00681412.pdf
- **Excerpt:** "Thus, the conventional Shannon limit of about 36 kb/s can be beaten, and it is possible to approach the digital transmission rate of 64 kb/s."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that the paper says this. It is an estimate that depends on the assumed SNR and bandwidth.

### 26. ESTIMATE: Other sources give about 35 kbit/s for the same limit.
- **Source A:** Brent Townshend, US Patent 5,801,695, description. https://patents.google.com/patent/US5801695A/en
- **Excerpt A:** "One can show that, based only on these values, it is not possible to transmit data at rates greater than approximately 35,000 bits/second."
- **Source B:** Frank Gao, "An Introduction to the V.90 (56K) Modem", EE Times, 15 Dec. 1998. https://www.eetimes.com/an-introduction-to-the-v-90-56k-modem/
- **Excerpt B:** "modems were theoretically limited to about 35 Kbps by the quantization noise that affects analog to digital conversions (the practical limit was actually 33.6 Kbps)."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that the sources say it; the number itself is an estimate (see Disputed D7).

### 27. Forney et al. give the channel figures behind such estimates: on a mostly digital connection the limit is PCM quantization noise and band-limiting filters.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 28.
- **Excerpt:** "bandlimiting to about 3700 Hz by linear anti-aliasing and interpolation filters."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (the neighbouring line that gives SNR "of the order of 34-38 dB" is partly lost in the OCR)

### 28. 56k modems do not break Shannon's theorem. The New York Times printed a correction on this point.
- **Source:** Correction (15 Oct. 1996) attached to "Keeping Up With the Phoneses", The New York Times, 24 Sept. 1996, Personal Computers column (byline not visible in the copy read). Read via http://web.archive.org/web/20231230225052/https://www.nytimes.com/1996/09/24/science/keeping-up-with-the-phoneses.html
- **Excerpt:** "(The law mathematically described a limit, for the conventional public telephone network. of about 35,000 bits per second.) [...] They will not circumvent Shannon's Law."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

## 4. 33.6k

### 29. The ITU revision that raised V.34 to 33,600 bit/s was approved 18 October 1996. The title changed from "up to 28 800" to "up to 33 600".
- **Source:** ITU-T, V.34 (10/96) edition page. https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.34-199610-S/en
- **Excerpt:** "Recommendation V.34 (10/96) Approved in 1996-10-18 Status : Superseded"
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 30. 33,600 and 31,200 bit/s are optional rates in V.34. The current edition is dated February 1998.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.34 (02/98), clause 1 and "Source" paragraph (PDF as claim 12).
- **Excerpt:** "ITU-T Recommendation V.34 was revised by ITU-T Study Group 16 (1997-2000) and was approved under the WTSC Resolution No. 1 procedure on the 6th of February 1998."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (clause 1 lists "33 600 bit/s (Optional)" and "31 200 bit/s (Optional)")

### 31. The step to 33.6k was a larger constellation, not a new modulation.
- **Source:** Forney et al. 1996, p. 30.
- **Excerpt:** "accomplished simply by increasing the maximum constellation size to 1664 points."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 32. U.S. Robotics shipped 31.2/33.6k software for Courier modems by April 1996 — about six months before the ITU approved the revision.
- **Source:** U.S. Robotics, "Courier V.Everything External Modem: Getting Started", "FINAL 4/96". https://support.usr.com/support/s-cour/s-cour-docs/1024492.pdf
- **Excerpt:** "The Courier contains software that enables 31.2 and 33.6 Kbps connection rates, as well as improves throughput at lower speeds."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (vendor manual with a printed date)

### 33. A trade report said USR was "first" with 33.6k software and that it tracked an ITU text not yet agreed.
- **Source:** "US Robotics' software speeds its Courier modem to 33.6Kbps", Computer Business Review (cbronline.com). The archived page shows no date. http://web.archive.org/web/20210117065329/https://www.cbronline.com/news/us_robotics_software_speeds_its_courier_modem_to_336kbps/
- **Excerpt:** "While the International Telecommunications Union has yet to agree the standard, the V.34 working group has now agreed on the enhancements needed to boost the speed, and the firm said its software closely tracks this work."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM for the report; LOW for "first" (vendor claim, undated page, no independent check — see Disputed D8)

### 34. The 1996 press called the 33.6k revision "V.34 bis" and judged the gain small.
- **Source A:** The New York Times, 24 Sept. 1996 (as claim 28).
- **Excerpt A:** "Earlier this year a new but not universally adopted modem standard called V.34 bis emerged, offering data transmission speeds of roughly 33,600 bits (33.6 kilobits) per second."
- **Source B:** Manes, The New York Times, 2 April 1996 (as claim 8).
- **Excerpt B:** "A recent addition to the V.34 protocol includes a new 33.6 kbps data rate, which offers a measly 17 percent improvement at best."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM. "V.34 bis" is press usage; the ITU never issued a Recommendation with that name (the ITU list shows only editions of V.34).

### 35. 33.6k needed a very clean line; 28.8k was reached on only part of the US network.
- **Source A:** Ayanoglu et al. 1998, p. 764.
- **Excerpt A:** "V.34, which can achieve 28.8 kb/s over an appreciable fraction of the U.S. subscriber loop ensemble. With some additional techniques, this can be increased to about 33.6 kb/s on very good connections."
- **Source B:** USR Courier manual 4/96 (as claim 32).
- **Excerpt B:** "V.34 connections at 21.6, 24.0, and 26.4 Kbps are common. To get connections of 28.8, 31.2, and 33.6 Kbps, line quality must be pristine end-to-end."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

## 5. 56k

### 36. The key condition: the ISP end is digital, so the path has only one conversion between digital and analog.
- **Source:** ITU press release, "Agreement reached on 56K Modem standard", Geneva, 6 Feb. 1998. http://web.archive.org/web/20051213230246/http://www.itu.int:80/newsarchive/press_releases/1998/04.html
- **Excerpt:** "V.90 modems are designed for connections which are digital at one end and have only one digital-to-analogue conversion."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 37. The central idea: use the telephone codec's own quantization levels as the signal alphabet, so quantization adds no noise.
- **Source:** Ayanoglu et al. 1998, p. 764.
- **Excerpt:** "The central idea behind the PCM modem is to avoid the effects of quantization distortion by utilizing the [µ]-law quantization levels themselves as the channel symbol alphabet."
- **Confidence:** HIGH (the "µ" is dropped in the PDF text layer)

### 38. V.90 downstream is PCM at 8000 symbols/s, 28,000–56,000 bit/s in steps of 8000/6 bit/s. Upstream is V.34.
- **Source:** ITU-T Rec. V.90 (09/98), clause 1. PDF: https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-V.90-199809-I!!PDF-E&type=items
- **Excerpt:** "c) PCM modulation in the downstream direction at a symbol rate of 8000; d) synchronous channel data signalling rates in the downstream direction from 28 000 bit/s to 56 000 bit/s in increments of 8000/6 bit/s;"
- **Confidence:** HIGH. The next item in the same list reads "e) V.34 modulation in the upstream direction;"

### 39. Why upstream stayed at V.34 speeds: the subscriber's signal still meets an analog-to-digital converter at the central office.
- **Source:** Gao, EE Times, 15 Dec. 1998 (as claim 26).
- **Excerpt:** "an analog-to-digital conversion and the associated quantization noise cannot be avoided in the upstream direction. This means that V.34 modulation techniques must be used and upstream speeds are still limited to 33.6 Kbps."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 40. Academic precedent: Kalet, Mazo and Saltzberg (named by Ayanoglu et al. as members of the Bell Labs PCM modem team) presented "The Capacity of PCM Voiceband Channels" at IEEE ICC '93 in Geneva, pp. 507–511.
- **Source A (bibliographic):** Crossref record, DOI 10.1109/ICC.1993.397316. https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/ICC.1993.397316
- **Source B (content, quoted by a later patent):** Townshend, US 5,801,695 (as claim 26).
- **Excerpt B:** "Kalet et al. state that "This is a hard practical problem and we can only conjecture if a reasonable solution would be possible." Id. at page 510."
- **Confidence:** HIGH for the paper's existence, authors, venue and pages. MEDIUM for its content: I did not open the paper. Its IEEE Xplore abstract ("at least 56 kbps (for a 3.5 kHz baseband bandwidth)") was seen only as a search-result snippet.

### 41. Bell Labs' account: a PCM modem prototype was demonstrated there in 1993, and R. W. Lucky first saw the potential.
- **Source:** Ayanoglu et al. 1998, p. 764 and Acknowledgment.
- **Excerpt:** "it was R. W. Lucky who first recognized the potential of transmission at speeds higher than the Shannon limit in the digital PSTN."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that the paper says this. It is the Bell Labs team's own account (see Disputed D1). The paper also says the prototype "was developed and demonstrated at Bell Laboratories in 1993."

### 42. AT&T filed patents on codec-synchronized modems from 1992: US 5,394,437 (filed 20 Oct. 1992, issued 28 Feb. 1995), US 5,406,583 (filed 21 June 1993), US 5,528,625 (filed 3 Jan. 1994).
- **Source:** Google Patents records. https://patents.google.com/patent/US5394437A/en , https://patents.google.com/patent/US5406583A/en , https://patents.google.com/patent/US5528625A/en
- **Excerpt (US 5,394,437 abstract):** "To minimize quantization noise, the modem's output is conditioned to minimize intersymbol interference by adjusting the modem's output to the A/D converter's sampling times and slicing levels."
- **Confidence:** HIGH for dates, inventors (Ayanoglu, Dagdeviren, Mazo, Saltzberg; Dagdeviren; Ayanoglu, Golden, Jones, Mazo, Shaw) and assignee. I did not analyse the claims.

### 43. Brent Townshend's patent US 5,801,695 was issued 1 Sept. 1998. It is a continuation of an application filed 9 Dec. 1994. A second patent, US 5,809,075, was issued 15 Sept. 1998.
- **Source:** Google Patents. https://patents.google.com/patent/US5801695A/en , https://patents.google.com/patent/US5809075A/en
- **Excerpt:** "This is a continuation of application Ser. No. 08/352,651, filed Dec. 9, 1994, now abandoned."
- **Confidence:** HIGH

### 44. Townshend's patent claims the asymmetric arrangement (one end digital) as the step that removes the old limit, and treats the earlier Bell Labs paper as an unproven conjecture.
- **Source:** Townshend, US 5,801,695, abstract and background.
- **Excerpt:** "By using a novel asymmetric configuration of the two communication endpoints, the currently-accepted maximum theoretical limits on the data rate are no longer applicable."
- **Confidence:** HIGH that the patent says this

### 45. Townshend's own account (2004): the idea came in 1993 while he built "Music Fax", a box to download music over direct-dial phone lines.
- **Source:** Tim Greene, "More than just a modem man", Network World, 8 March 2004. https://www.networkworld.com/article/2331175/more-than-just-a-modem-man.html
- **Excerpt:** "he came up with the idea in 1993 while building an appliance for downloading music from servers over direct-dial phone connections."
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (profile based on interviews; see Disputed D2 for conflicting dates)

### 46. Townshend licensed U.S. Robotics in April 1996. 3Com (USR's owner by then) made this public in September 1997 with a price list: $1.25 per client modem, $9 per ISP port.

## Disputed / uncertain

### D1. Who invented the 56k modem? — DISPUTED
- **Version A (Townshend / 3Com):** Townshend "conceived the original idea". Source: 3Com statement as reported by Wired, 5 Sept. 1997: "Townshend, the company says "conceived the original idea for how these new 56-Kbps modems operate."" An IDC analyst in the same article said Townshend "owns the core 56-Kbps intellectual property". His patent (US 5,801,695) says the earlier Bell Labs proposal "has never been reduced to practice". He later won licences from most of the industry, including Lucent and Conexant (EE Times, 3 Dec. 2003).
- **Version B (Bell Labs / Lucent):** Bell Labs authors say the capacity result (Kalet, Mazo, Saltzberg, ICC '93) and its practical use "was recognized early on", that a prototype "was developed and demonstrated at Bell Laboratories in 1993", and they credit R. W. Lucky with first seeing the potential (Ayanoglu et al., IEEE Trans. Commun., June 1998). AT&T patent filings start on 20 Oct. 1992 (US 5,394,437) — two years before Townshend's first application (9 Dec. 1994). Wired, 5 Sept. 1997: "Lucent Technologies believes it has relevant patents that could predate the Townshend claims, Pelgrim says."
- **Version C (Rockwell):** Rockwell denied using Townshend's information and called the pending patent "too broad from what we know of it" (Wired, 5 Sept. and 23 Oct. 1997). It then settled in 2001 (Network World, 8 Dec. 2003).
- **What the sources support:** The 1993 Bell Labs paper and the Bell Labs patents describe signalling on codec quantization levels. Townshend's patent stresses the asymmetric case (one end digital) and a working implementation. The Bell Labs 1998 paper says the practicality of an analog/digital configuration "was recognized early on" and cites the three AT&T patents for that; I did not analyse the patent claims to confirm it. No court ruling on validity or priority was found. Present both; do not write "the inventor" without attribution.

### D2. When did Townshend invent it? — sources conflict
- "came up with the idea in 1993" — Network World, 8 March 2004.
- First patent application 9 Dec. 1994 — US 5,801,695.
- "says that in 1995, he presented Rockwell with his technology" — Wired, 23 Oct. 1997. "3Com says Townshend initially approached the company in 1995" — Wired, 5 Sept. 1997.
- "created a core concept in 1996" — Computer History Museum catalogue record for his 2022 oral history (https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102792697).
- "since Townshend announced in 1997" — Network World, 8 Dec. 2003.
- Use the patent filing date (Dec. 1994) as the firm date and attribute the 1993 date to Townshend's own account.

### D3. Is the US cap 52, 53, 53.3 or 54 kbit/s?
- 54 kbps — FCC 98-221 (claim 59).
- 53K — USR User's Guide, Sept. 2000 (claim 59); "about 53 K" — J. D. Biersdorfer, "Q & A; Speed Limits On Modems", The New York Times, 11 June 1998 (http://web.archive.org/web/20170509115449/http://www.nytimes.com:80/1998/06/11/technology/q-a-speed-limits-on-modems.html); "53,000 bps" — Wired, 17 Sept. 1998 (https://www.wired.com/1998/09/fcc-to-give-modems-extra-kick/).
- "around 52 kilobits per second" — Dunn, NYT, 25 March 1997.
- "53.3 Kbps" — seen only in a search-result snippet of a later USR datasheet; not opened. My own arithmetic (not from a source): V.90 rates are 28,000 + n × 8000/6, so 53,333 and 54,667 are both valid rates; this would explain "53.3" and "54" as neighbouring steps. Treat that as inference.

### D4. Was the FCC power limit ever relaxed?
- The FCC proposed −6 dBm in Sept. 1998 (claims 58, 60). I found no Report and Order in Docket 98-163. A USR manual dated Sept. 2000 still states the 53K cap. A keyword search of the FCC's Nov. 2000 Part 68 order (CC Docket 99-216, https://part68.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/fcc_part68RO.doc, converted to text) found no mention of Docket 98-163, "−12 dBm" or PCM modems. Status: unknown from the sources read.

### D5. Why 56k and not 64k? — three explanations in the sources
- Network use of one bit: FCC 98-221 note 7: "only seven of the eight bits in a word are available to carry information, with the eighth budgeted for network use." Townshend's patent says the same of the low-order bit, reducing the rate "to 56,000 bits/second".
- Noise near zero: Dunn, NYT 1997: "because the low-end voltages are often indistinguishable from normal line noise, the modems end up using 128 voltage steps".
- Coding choice: Gao, EE Times 1998: "each symbol is maximally coded from 7 bits of each 8-bit pulse code modulation (PCM) word."
- These are not the same claim. The ITU text only states the 56,000 bit/s maximum.

### D6. Dates of V.90 "approval"
- 6 Feb. 1998 was "determination", not final approval (ITU press release). Wired the same day said the ITU "is formally adopting a standard today" — loose wording.
- Final approval: 25 Sept. 1998 (V.90 PDF and ITU database).
- CNET on 17 Sept. 1998 reported "yesterday's announcement of formal ratification of the standard" — nine days before the ITU's stated date. The sources read do not explain the gap.
- The ITU edition page (https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-V.90-199809-I/en) shows "Approved in 1998-09-01", and the V.34 (02/98) page shows "1998-02-01" while its PDF says 6 Feb. 1998. The "-01" days look like placeholders; prefer the PDF text.

### D7. The "Shannon limit" of a phone line: 30k, 33.6k, 35k or 36k?
- "approaching 30,000 bits per second" as near the maximum — Townshend patent background.
- 33.6k "very near the theoretical top speed" — NYT, 24 Sept. 1996 (then corrected to "about 35,000").
- "approximately 35,000" — Townshend patent; EE Times 1998.
- "about 36 kb/s" — Ayanoglu et al. 1998.
- All are estimates for assumed SNR and bandwidth. The sources agree that the limiting noise on a mostly digital network is PCM quantization, and that 56k works by removing that noise in one direction, not by beating Shannon.

### D8. Who shipped 33.6k first?
- Computer Business Review said USR "is first" (claim 33) — a report of a vendor announcement on an undated page. The USR manual dated 4/96 proves USR had it by April 1996. I found no independent ranking of vendors, so "first" stays unconfirmed.

### D9. Who gets credit for shell mapping?
- Forney et al. 1996 credit "many authors [8-12]" and name no company. The first reference is Lang & Longstaff, "A Leech Lattice Modem", IEEE JSAC, Aug. 1989. The paper does not give their employer. Do not credit a single inventor or "Motorola Codex" on the basis of the sources read.

### D10. When was V.34 "done": June or September 1994?
- Walsh (March 1994) expected the standard to be finalized "in June". Rockwell (Aug. 1993, via Telecompaper) expected ratification in June 1994. Forney et al.: "completed in the summer of 1994". ITU approval: 20 Sept. 1994. The date of the technical agreement before formal approval was not found.

### D11. Order of the 1996 announcements
- Rockwell first (September): CNET, 16 Oct. 1996: vendors "threw their weight in September behind a different 56-kpbs modem chipset from Rockwell"; Moore 1997: Rockwell's white paper came first, then USR.
- USR first to announce a product formally (16 Oct. 1996, CNET) and first to ship (Feb. 1997).
- The exact date of the Rockwell–Lucent K56flex announcement was not verified (search snippets gave 15 Nov. 1996 and "December 1996"). An Electronic Products item (page marked "JAN", https://www.electronicproducts.com/chip-maker-leads-effort-to-develop-faster-modems/) reports it as recent.

### D12. V.90 upstream rate and an error in one source
- ITU V.90: upstream up to 28,800, optional 31,200 and 33,600. USR's 2000 manual says its modems send "at 31.2 Kbps". Network World (8 Dec. 2003) prints "Upload speeds for the modems is 38.6K bit/sec" — not supported by any other source read; treat as an error.

### D13. Royalty terms differ between reports
- 1997 (Wired): $1.25 per client modem, $9 per ISP port. 2003 (Network World): "$1.25 per PC modem and $2.50 per server modem". Both may be right for different dates; not reconciled.
